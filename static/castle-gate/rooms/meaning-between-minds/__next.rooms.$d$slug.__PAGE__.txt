2:T1727,<p>They mostly don't — not the way the question pictures it. The image built into &quot;carry meaning&quot; is what linguist Michael Reddy named the <em>conduit metaphor</em>: I load a thought into words, send them across, and you unpack the same thought. Reddy argued this is largely false. Words are not containers; they are prompts. The listener rebuilds the meaning from their own materials, and the rebuilt version may or may not match the original.</p>
<p>Three quiet failures show the gap. People disagree about what ordinary words even mean — one study estimated 10 to 30 distinct variants for common nouns, with two strangers agreeing only 13–70% of the time — yet nearly everyone assumed about two-thirds of others shared their meaning. Closeness does not fix this: spouses decoding each other's ambiguous phrases were no more accurate than strangers, just more confident. And we read &quot;good enough&quot; rather than exactly, so we misread even simple, clear sentences when a wrong reading seems more plausible.</p>
<p>So is nothing shared? No — something real is. When two people genuinely communicate, the listener's brain activity comes to mirror the speaker's, and the better listeners are the ones whose brains <em>anticipate</em> what is coming. Crucially, that coupling vanishes the moment understanding breaks — a story told in a language you don't speak produces no sync. Meaning, then, is not a property of the words; it is a conditional achievement the two brains have to produce together.</p>
<p>That is the real answer: shared meaning is <em>built</em>, not sent. Conversation works because people constantly align — unconsciously echoing each other's words and sentence shapes — and because they <em>ground</em>: nodding, saying &quot;uh-huh,&quot; asking &quot;wait, you mean Friday?&quot; Across twelve languages, people interrupt to repair understanding about once every 90 seconds. So clarifying is not pedantry or a sign of failure; it is the mechanism. Words don't carry meaning between minds. They give two minds enough to build something close to the same thing — if both keep working at it.</p>
<p>In castle words: the founder's founding stone says words and their meanings are the <a href="/castle-gate/words/bridge">bridge</a> of understanding — and this research adds that the bridge stands on <a href="/castle-gate/words/common-ground">common-ground</a> (Herbert Clark's name for everything two people believe they both know) and is kept standing by constant small repairs.</p>
<h2>Which words need almost no rebuilding</h2>
<p>The first door this room opened — why do some words rebuild cleanly while others need constant repair? — answers like this: the rebuilding cost tracks how far a word stands from the shared senses.</p>
<p>Words whose referents the senses can check — &quot;apple&quot;, &quot;thunder&quot;, &quot;table&quot; — rebuild almost identically in every mind, because both builders work from the same materials: eyes, hands, weather. Concrete words are processed faster, remembered better, and leave a smaller margin of meaning error precisely because their referent can be pointed at; abstract words like &quot;freedom&quot; or &quot;ethics&quot; have no common object to check against, so each mind raises them from its own history, and they drift (<a href="https://www.ifioque.com/linguistic/concrete_vs_abstract_words" rel="noopener">Concrete vs. abstract words, Ifioque</a>, read 2026-06-10; on the deeper grounding of concrete language in the body, <a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00227/full" rel="noopener">Borghi &amp; Cimatti, Frontiers in Psychology 2011</a>, read 2026-06-10).</p>
<p>At the far end stand what W. B. Gallie in 1956 named <a href="/castle-gate/words/essentially-contested-concept">essentially-contested-concept</a>s — &quot;democracy&quot;, &quot;art&quot;, &quot;social justice&quot; — words whose proper use <em>inevitably</em> involves endless dispute among their users, with no standard use all sides could settle on (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_contested_concept" rel="noopener">Essentially contested concept, Wikipedia</a>, read 2026-06-10). For these words, repair is not a failure state; it is the word's permanent condition — the arguing is part of what the word is for.</p>
<p>So the ladder runs: pointable things need almost no repair, abstractions need regular repair, and contested ideals are repair all the way down. A practical rule for this castle: the more abstract the word, the more its file must earn its keep.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>Almost all of this is mechanism and tendency, not guarantee. The neural-coupling work is small and correlational — brains syncing during understanding is not proof that &quot;meaning&quot; itself moves, and you cannot will the sync on. The word-by-word brain-to-brain study used just 5 conversing pairs and leaned on a language model as a stand-in for the shared code, not the brain's actual one. The famous couples result used contrived lab phrases. And the picture is genuinely contested: Nasiadka (2012) defends the conduit metaphor against Reddy, and the &quot;good-enough processing&quot; idea has been faulted in a 20-year review as too vague to falsify. Neither the transmission view nor its critique is settled.</p>
