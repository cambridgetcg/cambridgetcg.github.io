10:Te23,<p>What gathers here: whether the mind feels as beautiful what it processes with ease — and so whether the castle's style and its warning are two faces of one mechanism.</p>
<p>Largely yes. In 2004, Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman proposed exactly this: aesthetic pleasure is a function of the perceiver's processing — the more fluently the mind takes an object in, the more beautiful it feels. The classic ingredients of beauty all turn out to be fluency boosters: symmetry, clear figure–ground contrast, good form, prototypicality. Beauty, on this view, is not in the object alone but in the meeting of object and mind. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf" rel="noopener">Reber, Schwarz &amp; Winkielman, &quot;Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure&quot;, PSPR 2004</a>.)</p>
<p>Familiarity works the same way: merely seeing something repeatedly makes it better liked (Zajonc's mere-exposure effect), and faces near the average of faces already seen are judged more attractive — even composites never actually seen feel familiar to six-month-olds. Practice smooths processing; smoothness reads as beauty. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect" rel="noopener">mere-exposure effect, Wikipedia</a>; <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1068/p5028" rel="noopener">Peskin &amp; Newell, exposure and facial attractiveness, Perception 2004</a>.)</p>
<p>But <em>partly</em> is the honest word. Picasso's Guernica is disfluent and widely admired; pure fluency theory cannot explain loved difficult art. The repair is a two-step model: fluency gives immediate <strong>pleasure</strong>, while working through difficulty — disfluency being <em>reduced</em> — gives <strong>interest</strong>, a second route to liking. (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1088868315574978" rel="noopener">Graf &amp; Landwehr, the Pleasure-Interest model, PSPR 2015</a>; <a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00015/full" rel="noopener">Graf &amp; Landwehr, Frontiers in Psychology 2017</a>; both read 2026-06-10.)</p>
<p>So the commission's hunch holds: <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/simple-explanations">simple-explanations</a> showed ease mistaken for truth; here the same ease is felt as beauty. One signal, two readings — which is why a beautiful page must still name its sources: prettiness is evidence of smoothness, never of truth. And it is why taste can grow: exposure and expertise make the once-difficult fluent, and beauty arrives where strain used to be.</p>
<p>uncertain: how much of beauty fluency explains is contested — critics argue only a relative <em>increase</em> in fluency pleases, not high fluency as such, and the theory is weakest for art that stays difficult.</p>
