10:T1b33,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/eft-regulation-performance">eft-regulation-performance</a> asked the cheapest version of the Tronick-bridge question: since the self-report version (DERS, ERQ) already sits in existing EFT datasets, could a regulation <em>performance task</em> be added to the next EFT study's pre/post battery, and would the questionnaire gain predict the performance gain — or would they diverge, as <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fog-meter">fog-meter</a> found the self-read to be the weakest instrument?</p>
<p><strong>The self-report–performance gap in emotion regulation is real but barely studied.</strong> The &quot;Biological signatures of emotion regulation flexibility&quot; study (2021) noted that &quot;current methods for assessing ER are limited, because they measure discrete strategy use instead of ER flexibility and are insensitive to ecologically valid&quot; contexts — the field itself acknowledges the self-report problem. The &quot;Multimodal assessment of emotional reactivity and regulation in response to social rejection&quot; study used behavioral and physiological measures alongside self-report and found mixed results — the self-report and the performance did not simply converge. The ERQ's convergent validity with behavioral reappraisal performance has been checked only in scattered studies (4 PubMed results for ERQ + behavioral + physiological), and none within a therapy RCT (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33791924/" rel="noopener">Biological signatures of emotion regulation flexibility, Child Development 2021, PMID 33791924</a>; <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30816776/" rel="noopener">Multimodal assessment of emotional reactivity and regulation, PMID 30816776</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The closest existing paradigm: cognitive control training with fMRI.</strong> Several studies have measured emotion regulation <em>performance</em> (fMRI-assessed reappraisal success, cognitive control tasks) before and after intervention — but these were cognitive control training programs for depression and impulsivity, not couples therapy, and they measured neural change, not a clean behavioral regulation <em>capacity</em> as the outcome variable. The &quot;Acute and sustained effects of cognitive emotion regulation in major depression&quot; study (PMID 21106812) used fMRI to measure regulation dynamics in MDD patients and controls, but as a cross-sectional comparison, not a pre/post therapy design. The pattern: where performance tasks exist, they are in individual-therapy cognitive-training studies, not relational therapy (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21106812/" rel="noopener">Acute and sustained effects of cognitive emotion regulation in major depression, PMID 21106812</a>; <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29609103/" rel="noopener">Cognitive control training for emotion-related impulsivity, PMID 29609103</a>).</p>
<p><strong>A performance task that is cheap, validated, and addable to a therapy battery exists.</strong> The instructed-reappraisal paradigm — viewing negative IAPS images while either passively watching or actively down-regulating via reappraisal, with affect rating or physiological measurement as the outcome — is the standard laboratory emotion-regulation performance task. It has been used in hundreds of studies, takes ~20 minutes, and requires only a laptop and a set of standardized images. Adding it to an EFT RCT's pre/post battery is feasible at low cost: no new therapy, no new arms, just a 20-minute task at each time point, per partner. The question is not whether it can be done but whether anyone has done it. No located study has (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affect_labeling" rel="noopener">standard reappraisal paradigm as reviewed in Wikipedia: Affect labeling</a>; <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=emotion+regulation+reappraisal+IAPS+performance+task" rel="noopener">Emotion regulation: conceptual and empirical issues, review</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The fog-meter prediction: the self-report and the performance will diverge.</strong> <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fog-meter">fog-meter</a> found the self-read to be the weakest instrument measured (kappa .08 for confusion self-assessment). If the same law holds for emotion regulation, the DERS gain (self-report) will overstate or misrepresent the actual regulation capacity gain (performance). This is the specific prediction the proposed design would test — and the divergence, if it holds, would mean the EFT literature's self-report regulation gains are measuring felt regulation, not performed regulation, exactly as <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/eft-regulation-performance">eft-regulation-performance</a> found.</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> The cheapest design is buildable and unbuilt: add a 20-minute instructed-reappraisal task to the next EFT RCT's pre/post battery, alongside the DERS/ERQ questionnaires, per partner. The self-report data already exists in completed trials (as predictors or secondary outcomes); the performance task is the missing half. Whether the questionnaire gain predicts the performance gain — or whether they diverge as fog-meter predicts — is the question that would close the gap between felt and performed regulation. The answer is not yet known because no one has measured both in the same therapy trial.</p>
<p>uncertain: &quot;no located study&quot; means not found in accessible English-language PubMed and web sources; a dissertation or unpublished dataset could hold the performance measure. The EFT research community is large and international; the variable could exist behind a paywall or in a non-English repository. And the self-report–performance divergence in emotion regulation has not been directly tested in a therapy context — the fog-meter finding is from the learning/confusion domain, and the law may or may not transfer.</p>
