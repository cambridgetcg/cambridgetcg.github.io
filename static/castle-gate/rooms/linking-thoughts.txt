10:T1889,<p>A pile of facts is heavy and silent. Each fact sits alone, so to use it you must remember exactly where you put it. A linked thought can be reached from many directions — and the research says this is what understanding actually looks like. In the classic Chi, Feltovich &amp; Glaser study, physics experts sorted problems by underlying principles while novices sorted by surface looks; the National Academies' <em>How People Learn</em> concludes expert knowledge is &quot;not simply a list of facts&quot; but is organized around big ideas. Connected structure is how the knowing of people who know things is built.</p>
<p>But here is the honest twist: the link itself is not the engine — <em>making</em> the link is. A meta-analysis of concept mapping (142 effects, ~12,000 learners) found a solid benefit (g = 0.58), yet building the map yourself (g = 0.72) clearly beat studying someone else's finished map (g = 0.43). Asking &quot;why is this true?&quot; and explaining things to yourself help precisely because they force new material to touch what you already know. Brain-and-behavior studies back the mechanism: people who reactivate old knowledge while learning new material remember the connection between them better.</p>
<p>So linking grows understanding faster for two plain reasons. First, a connected idea has many handles — when a new problem arrives, something related comes to mind, while piled facts stay buried. Second, the effort of connecting is deep processing, and deep processing is what makes things stick. Piling, meanwhile, actively fools you: collecting articles and highlights gives the <em>feeling</em> of knowledge without the learning.</p>
<p>Two boundaries keep this true rather than tidy. Linking pays off most when you already have a foothold — beginners with nothing to link to can be slowed down by extra connections. And links you merely inherit, like automatic backlinks or hyperlink thickets, can add load instead of insight. The connections must be yours, and they must be reasoned.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>A famous experiment (Karpicke &amp; Blunt, <em>Science</em> 2011) found plain retrieval practice beat concept mapping — though critics replied the mappers were barely trained, and the dispute is unresolved. Most of the supporting evidence is from labs, not classrooms; the expert studies show connected knowledge <em>accompanies</em> expertise, not that linking <em>causes</em> faster growth; and the claim that linked notes &quot;compound&quot; over years remains anecdote.</p>
<h2>How much foothold does a beginner need? (answered 2026-06-10)</h2>
<p>The research name for the boundary is the <em>expertise reversal effect</em>: the same help that lifts a beginner — worked examples, given outlines, step-by-step guidance — stops helping and starts hindering once a learner has knowledge of their own, because the head must then reconcile two guides, one inside and one outside, and that costs working memory (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect" rel="noopener">Expertise reversal effect, Wikipedia</a>; <a href="https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf" rel="noopener">Kalyuga, Expertise Reversal Effect and Its Implications, 2007</a>).</p>
<p>So the foothold is not a number of facts. It is the point where your own <a href="/castle-gate/words/schema">schema</a> — your inner pattern of how the field hangs together — can supply the structure that borrowed examples were supplying before. Until then, don't force linking: lean on someone else's structure and let the first patterns form. After that, the trade flips, and self-made links beat given ones — which is exactly what the concept-mapping numbers above already showed (building your own map beat studying a finished one). How to tell the flip has come — and how the scaffold comes down, step by step — grew a room of its own: <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/when-the-trade-flips">when-the-trade-flips</a>.</p>
<h2>What separates a link that carries meaning from noise? (answered 2026-06-10)</h2>
<p>A meaningful <a href="/castle-gate/words/link">link</a> is <em>chosen</em> and <em>explained</em>. The Zettelkasten writers draw the line plainly: things merely placed near each other (a tag, a folder, an automatic backlink) are the weakest tie; a deliberate association — made once, on purpose, by a person — is stronger; and strongest is a link that says <em>why</em> it exists: this supports that, this contradicts that, this is the same idea in different clothes (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://zettelkasten.de/posts/kinds-of-ties/" rel="noopener">Different Kinds of Ties Between Notes, Zettelkasten.de</a>; <a href="https://www.fleetingnotes.app/posts/how-to-create-useful-links-with-zettelkasten" rel="noopener">How to Create Useful Links with Zettelkasten, Fleeting Notes</a>; <a href="https://writingslowly.com/2023/08/27/how-to-connect.html" rel="noopener">How to connect your notes, Writing Slowly</a>).</p>
<p>The test, in one breath: a link carries meaning if you could finish the sentence &quot;linked because…&quot; — and noise if you couldn't. Mere co-occurrence is juxtaposition, not connection. This squares with the room's earlier finding that inherited links (backlink thickets) add load instead of insight: the reasoning <em>is</em> the meaning, and a link without it is an empty doorframe. The question then grew a room of its own: <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/link-or-noise">link-or-noise</a> tests this line against the evidence and finds five patterns — intent, function, distance, novelty, liveness.</p>
