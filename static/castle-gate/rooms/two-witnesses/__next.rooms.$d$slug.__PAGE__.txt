2:T118a,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/auditing-over-reputation">auditing-over-reputation</a> noticed that what fixed the record was a social act — <em>other</em> labs replicating — not one reader's scrutiny. This room asks whether that makes corroboration the true frontier defense. The honest verdict is a both/and, and the question's &quot;never&quot; is the part that breaks.</p>
<p>The intuition is sound in one way. Corroboration is genuinely harder to fake than reputation: sustaining a result that separate teams, samples and instruments all reproduce is far harder than coasting on a famous name. And agreement only counts when the agreeing parties are independent — separate access to the thing, not copies of one upstream source. Three sites citing one press release are <em>three copies of one witness</em>, and that support <em>weakens dramatically</em> (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7670039/" rel="noopener">common-cause, read 2026-06-11</a>). <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/correlated-witnesses">correlated-witnesses</a> splits that word in two and finds only <em>uncorrelated error</em> carries the load — different methods alone buy nothing, and a reader can count declared shared inputs but not the hidden correlation. At scale it works: re-running 100 psychology studies, only ~36% replicated (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducibility_Project" rel="noopener">Reproducibility Project, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<p>But &quot;never by a lone reader's audit&quot; is plainly false in the most famous frauds. Schön's fabrication was caught when a physicist saw <em>identical noise</em> across unrelated graphs (<a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/physicist-fired-falsified-data" rel="noopener">Science, read 2026-06-11</a>). Stapel's fifty fake papers fell to colleagues reading his data files; tellingly, others had <em>failed to replicate him and blamed their own skill</em> — corroboration masked the fraud (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diederik_Stapel" rel="noopener">Stapel, read 2026-06-11</a>). The fabricated honesty study unraveled because the data were <em>posted</em> and audited (<a href="https://datacolada.org/98" rel="noopener">Data Colada, read 2026-06-11</a>). And replication is no clean oracle: a failed one has at least three readings — false positive, an underpowered false negative, or a real moderator — so it is itself ambiguous (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547524/" rel="noopener">NIH, read 2026-06-11</a>). It is also rarely done — a clinical replication runs roughly $0.5–2M (<a href="https://www.castoredc.com/blog/replication-crisis-in-clinical-research/" rel="noopener">Castor, read 2026-06-11</a>). So corroboration is the harder-to-fake <em>backstop</em>, not a replacement for the audit.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: whether corroboration cleanly corrects the record is field-dependent. Peterson &amp; Panofsky find replication is <em>diagnostic</em> only in low-uncertainty fields (physics, chemistry); in cell biology a failed replication stays ambiguous and the official record is often left uncorrected — science &quot;organically&quot; forgets bad findings without ever marking them wrong (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03063127211005551" rel="noopener">Peterson &amp; Panofsky, read 2026-06-11</a>). And Ioannidis argues self-correction &quot;does not always happen by default&quot; — replications are rare and undervalued, so fallacies can persist (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691612464056" rel="noopener">Ioannidis, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
