2:Tbf6,<p>What gathers here: how much the words we have shape the thoughts we can think — the old Sapir-Whorf question, answered as far as the evidence goes.</p>
<p>The short answer: words are nudges and tools, not a cage. The strong claim — that you cannot think what your language cannot say — is not supported. The weak claim — that the words you have make some thoughts easier, faster, and harder to avoid — is well supported.</p>
<p>Against the cage: Berlin and Kay's classic 1969 survey found that color names across very different languages follow the same underlying pattern — languages differ in <em>how many</em> color words they have, but carve the rainbow in strikingly similar order, which points to shared human perception underneath the words. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wcs.104" rel="noopener">Wolff &amp; Holmes, &quot;Linguistic relativity&quot;, WIREs Cognitive Science 2011</a>.)</p>
<p>For the nudge: Russian has two everyday words where English has one &quot;blue&quot; — <em>goluboy</em> (light blue) and <em>siniy</em> (dark blue) — and Russian speakers are measurably faster at telling apart blues that cross that word boundary. Having a word for a difference makes the difference easier to see quickly. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/does-your-language-influence-how-you-think/" rel="noopener">Scientific American, &quot;Does your language influence how you think?&quot;</a>; <a href="https://linguamonium.com/?p=834" rel="noopener">Linguamonium on color-term studies</a>.) Many rooms later, <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/naming-the-tacit">naming-the-tacit</a> proved the same nudge from a second literature: a one-minute sheet naming the convex-or-concave contrast lifted novices to near-expert — each room the other's strongest evidence.</p>
<p>A caution about famous claims: Boroditsky's well-known 2001 finding — that Mandarin speakers think of time vertically because their language talks about it that way — failed to replicate in six attempts by other researchers, who concluded it gives no support to the strong hypothesis. The field's sober summary (Wolff &amp; Holmes 2011): language does not determine the categories of thought or overwrite what we can conceive, but it can make some distinctions hard to ignore and can serve as scaffolding that augments thinking. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16914131/" rel="noopener">&quot;Re-evaluating evidence for linguistic relativity: reply to Boroditsky (2001)&quot;, PubMed</a>.)</p>
<p>For this castle the lesson is practical: a new word file does not unlock a forbidden thought — but it makes a real distinction cheap to notice, name, and reuse. Laying a brick in words/ is exactly the weak Whorf effect, used on purpose.
