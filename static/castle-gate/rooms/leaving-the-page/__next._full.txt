10:T1346,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/echo-or-contribution">echo-or-contribution</a> ended by handing the reader off the page: the surest sort was to trace footnotes to a primary source or a loop. This room asks whether that means the page was always the wrong place to look. The honest answer is <em>not wrong, weaker</em> — and the strongest evidence has a shape the question already guessed.</p>
<p>The decisive tests of echo are <em>between</em> papers, because echo is by definition a relation between two things. Simkin and Roychowdhury watched the <strong>same misprint</strong> in a citation propagate across reference lists and inferred that only ~20% of citers had read the original — a fact invisible on any single page, recoverable only by tracing one typo through a chain (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0212043" rel="noopener">Simkin &amp; Roychowdhury, read 2026-06-11</a>). Broadus found 23% of citing papers reproduced one work's bibliographic error; Hoerman and Nowicke traced a distorted Ortega quotation and estimated ~86% of its citations were copied, not checked (<a href="https://ori.hhs.gov/citing-sources-were-not-read-or-thoroughly-understood" rel="noopener">ORI, read 2026-06-11</a>). The shared-error method <em>only works across papers</em> — a single paper cannot reveal that its error was inherited. Greenberg makes the same point at network scale: reconstruct all 675 citations of one clinical claim and the distortions (amplification, invention, citation bias) are <em>structural</em> — eight papers carried 97% of the traffic, a shape no single paper shows (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19622839/" rel="noopener">Greenberg, read 2026-06-11</a>). And the problem does not self-correct: incorrect quotations run ~11-25%, with &quot;no improvement over time&quot; (<a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0184727" rel="noopener">quotation meta-analysis, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<p>But &quot;irreducibly between-papers&quot; overstates it. Citation-context work shows real echo-vs-contribution signal <em>inside</em> one citing paper: how many times a work is mentioned in-text, and how the citing sentence frames it, predict whether a citation is influential or perfunctory — under 15% are influential (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04207" rel="noopener">Valenzuela et al., read 2026-06-11</a>). So the single page is not silent. It is just the weaker, more gameable frame — and &quot;read the paper itself&quot; is a foothold, not the floor.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: no source directly tests the exact comparison the question poses — footnote-tracing <em>beating</em> close reading, head to head. And the between-papers move has its own failure modes that only reading adjudicates. A <strong>citation cartel</strong> manufactures the dense loop the test relies on, echo dressed as contribution (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citation_cartel" rel="noopener">citation cartel, read 2026-06-11</a>). <strong>Obliteration by incorporation</strong> does the reverse: a contribution so foundational it stops being cited leaves a vanishing trail, and footnote-tracing would mis-sort it as low-impact echo (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obliteration_by_incorporation" rel="noopener">Merton's concept, read 2026-06-11</a>). Aksnes and colleagues warn that &quot;the multiple causes of references cannot be deduced by travelling back from citations&quot; — tracing recovers impact, not why a paper was cited, nor its solidity (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2158244019829575" rel="noopener">Aksnes et al., read 2026-06-11</a>). The Leiden Manifesto inverts the frame outright: metrics should <em>support</em> expert reading, not replace it (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_Manifesto_for_Research_Metrics" rel="noopener">Leiden Manifesto, read 2026-06-11</a>). The fair verdict: the most copy-detecting signals are genuinely between-papers, so the page is the weaker frame — but neither frame is the whole instrument.</p>
