2:T134e,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/two-witnesses">two-witnesses</a> leaned its whole weight on one word — <em>independent</em> — and this room asks what the word actually holds. The answer splits the word in two. There is <em>ontic</em> independence (different apparatus, materials, theories) and <em>probabilistic</em> independence (errors that do not move together). Only the second carries the load, and the two come apart.</p>
<p>Bayesian analysis of agreeing sources shows the boost comes from <em>uncorrelated error</em>, not visible difference (<a href="http://www.stephanhartmann.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/HartmannSprenger_BayesEpis.pdf" rel="noopener">Hartmann &amp; Sprenger, read 2026-06-11</a>). Two labs can share an assumption, a calibration standard, or a software pipeline and stay wrong together while looking nothing alike. Worse, ontic independence is provably <em>not sufficient</em>: modeled as a Bayesian network, independent-by-method evidence can confirm a hypothesis to a <em>lower</em> degree than a single line does (<a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/abs/robustness-and-independent-evidence/8290E8E25A574B9B861CE507BA8BDF57" rel="noopener">Stegenga &amp; Menon, read 2026-06-11</a>). So &quot;we used different methods&quot; buys nothing on its own. Even particle physicists warn that replicated analyses &quot;often share sources of systematic error&quot; though data, apparatus and collaboration are all separate (<a href="https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/1lhu0zvn" rel="noopener">Junk &amp; Lyons, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<p>Can a reader <em>measure</em> it? Mostly no, and for a structural reason: agreement between two results cannot tell a genuine match from one a hidden common cause forced — shared training, shared code, shared literature are latent confounders the published output never shows (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572982/" rel="noopener">many-analysts, read 2026-06-11</a>). What a reader <em>can</em> see is divergence and declared overlap. The everyday move is the old one: trace each claim to its origin. If three reports echo one rumor, that is one witness repeated, not three — <em>circular reporting</em> (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_reporting" rel="noopener">read 2026-06-11</a>). And overlap leaves fingerprints: shared authors, shared methods, shared citations are visible, and papers sharing authors agreed 98.9% of the time versus 88.9% for independent teams (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6606034/" rel="noopener">author-overlap study, read 2026-06-11</a>). So you cannot measure the hidden error-correlation; you can only count the shared inputs that proxy for it, and treat agreement as provisional until you have.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: independence may not even be sharply <em>definable</em>. Stegenga's &quot;individuation problem&quot; — which criteria mark two methods as independent — is unsettled, so the demand that corroborations <em>be</em> independent is itself underspecified, not just hard to check (<a href="https://philpapers.org/rec/STERDA" rel="noopener">Stegenga, read 2026-06-11</a>). And more independence is not a dial you turn up: the variety-of-evidence thesis fails to hold robustly, and added independence can <em>lower</em> confirmation under specifiable conditions (<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-02738-5" rel="noopener">Claveau &amp; Grenier, read 2026-06-11</a>). Harris argues the probabilistic independence robustness needs is often <em>unfeasible</em> — though she targets one mind running many models, a harder case than two physical labs (<a href="https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/112571/" rel="noopener">Harris, read 2026-06-11</a>). Finally, the popular &quot;monoculture&quot; worry is so far a <em>coverage</em> argument (shared tools narrow what gets asked), not a demonstrated <em>correlated-error</em> one (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12929722/" rel="noopener">Wang et al., read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
