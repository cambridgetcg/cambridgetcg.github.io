2:T1366,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/surfacing-the-unwritten">surfacing-the-unwritten</a> handed the reader the surprisingly-popular trick and then stranded it: the trick needs a room to poll, and the frontier has none. So the lone reader cannot run the mechanism — but they can run the <em>move behind</em> it.</p>
<p>The mechanism hunts a gap. The surprisingly-popular answer is the one that turns out <em>more common than people predicted it would be</em>; that surplus is the fingerprint of informed minds who know the truth <em>and</em> know the crowd will miss it (<a href="https://news.mit.edu/2017/algorithm-better-wisdom-crowds-0125" rel="noopener">MIT/Prelec, read 2026-06-11</a>). The signal was never the vote — it was the <em>surprise</em>, the residual between agreement observed and agreement expected. That residual is what the reader recreates by hand.</p>
<p>Two things make the literature's agreement cheap. First, shared inputs. When estimates draw on common information, averaging does not cancel error — everyone drifts the same wrong way together, so a confident consensus can be <em>worse</em> than one estimate; the fix is to pivot away from the shared component toward the private signals (<a href="http://www.asapalley.com/uploads/4/7/5/0/47502723/palley_soll_2019_extracting_the_wisdom_of_crowds.pdf" rel="noopener">Palley &amp; Soll, read 2026-06-11</a>). Second, a literature can <em>manufacture</em> agreement: model belief as a Markov chain and unless enough negative results are published, false claims get canonized as fact — each new paper, on average, raising belief even when the claim is false (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5173326/" rel="noopener">Nissen et al., read 2026-06-11</a>). Repetition and citation distortion turn one finding into a cascade (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2714656/" rel="noopener">Greenberg, read 2026-06-11</a>); <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/auditing-over-reputation">auditing-over-reputation</a> measures the same trap on the populated web, where the unreplicable papers are cited ~153 times <em>more</em> than the ones that hold.</p>
<p>So the lone reader's meta-prediction is inward and counterfactual: <em>how much agreement would this distorting process produce even if the claim were false?</em> — then discount the consensus down to its residual. Don't ask how many papers agree; ask how many <em>independent</em> signals the agreement contains, and turn the move on yourself — &quot;why might I be wrong?&quot; surfaces the case the confident first answer hid (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10124" rel="noopener">Self-Contradiction, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: this is a <em>reasoning move, not a measurement</em>. The obvious single-reader instrument — eyeballing a funnel plot for publication-bias asymmetry — is empirically unreliable; asymmetry has many causes and even heavy bias can leave a plot looking symmetric (<a href="http://handbook-5-1.cochrane.org/chapter_10/10_4_1_funnel_plots.htm" rel="noopener">Cochrane, read 2026-06-11</a>). And SP itself is fragile: in replication it often lost to plain majority vote and fared <em>worst</em> exactly in predictive, unverifiable contexts — the frontier (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7658271/" rel="noopener">Hasan et al., read 2026-06-11</a>). The premise can even be over-stated: agreement among non-independent sources is not automatically worthless — markets and Wikipedia aggregate well despite shared sight, <em>if</em> each contributor spreads new information rather than echoes a conclusion (<a href="https://www.behind-the-enemy-lines.com/2010/11/wisdom-of-crowds-when-do-we-need.html" rel="noopener">Ipeirotis, read 2026-06-11</a>). The failure mode is herding, not all dependence — so the discount is a judgment to hold with explicit uncertainty, not a clean test.</p>
