10:T102e,<p>The premise holds. One signal — the felt ease of processing — is read by the mind as beauty and as truth alike; a clean font, a familiar tune, a symmetric face all smooth the way, and the smoothness arrives as &quot;lovely&quot; and &quot;right&quot; in the same warm voice (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf" rel="noopener">Reber, Schwarz &amp; Winkielman, PSPR 2004</a>). So no single glance from inside can sort well-made from merely pretty: in that instant the feeling is the same feeling. But the feeling can be questioned, three ways, without leaving it.</p>
<p>First, trace the ease. The glow survives only while its source is hidden; the moment people can pin their ease on repetition or packaging, they discount it and it goes out (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processing_fluency_theory_of_aesthetic_pleasure" rel="noopener">processing fluency theory</a>). Repetition alone makes falsehoods feel true — even ones that contradict what you already know (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/xge-0000098.pdf" rel="noopener">Fazio et al.</a>). If the ease traces to the object's own structure, good; if to varnish, prettiness.</p>
<p>Second, watch which way the ease is moving. Merely pretty is easy at once and stays flat; well-made often resists a little, then clicks — and the click is the reward. Pleasure pays for smoothness, interest for difficulty resolved: complex works are liked <em>more</em> when meaning can be drawn from them (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11051202/" rel="noopener">PIA model evidence</a>), and experts feel interest before work that leaves novices only confused, because they can reach the simplicity underneath (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://tiffaning.com/files/Silvia_2013_Interested%20Experts,%20Confused%20Novices.pdf" rel="noopener">Silvia 2013</a>).</p>
<p>Third, re-meet it. Return tomorrow, with accuracy in mind. Loading the mind with another task dimmed felt beauty while sparing mere pleasantness — beauty seems to need thought (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096098221730427X" rel="noopener">Brielmann &amp; Pelli 2017</a>). The well-made holds or deepens on the third encounter; the merely pretty thins.</p>
<p>And the limit, said plainly: the signal is not self-validating. The ease-means-good link is learned, and can even be trained into reverse (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201563/" rel="noopener">Unkelbach 2007</a>). So the feeling can never be the judge. It can only call the witnesses — a flare that says <em>look closer</em>, never a verdict.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: no source offers one validated test; the three above are established mechanisms assembled, not a lab-proven procedure. The beauty-requires-thought result is contested — about half of viewers still called their chosen image beautiful under load. Making things artificially rough to sharpen judgment has failed to replicate; what reliably blocks false ease is an accuracy focus plus actual knowledge (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027719302276" rel="noopener">Nadarevic &amp; Erdfelder, Cognition 2019</a>). And some argue great work <em>begins</em> in deliberate disfluency (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4337024/" rel="noopener">Consoli</a>), so an ease-test misreads it early.</p>
