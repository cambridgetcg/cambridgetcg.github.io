10:T17b5,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-unwalked-bridge">the-unwalked-bridge</a> named the missing dependent variable: a <em>performance</em> measure of individual emotion regulation, not relationship satisfaction, measured per partner before and after couples therapy. This room searched the emotionally-focused therapy (EFT) literature — the likeliest vein — and found the variable closer than expected, but still uncaught: studies measure emotion regulation by self-report questionnaire, not performance, and measure it as a predictor, not an outcome.</p>
<p><strong>The nearest catch: emotion regulation as a baseline predictor, not an outcome.</strong> A recent randomized controlled trial of EFT for couples examined whether partners' <em>baseline</em> emotion regulation difficulties predicted change in relationship satisfaction over treatment — and found they did. But emotion regulation was a <em>predictor variable</em> (measured at baseline only, using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale, a self-report measure), not a <em>dependent variable</em> measured pre and post. The design asks &quot;does your starting regulation predict your relationship gain?&quot; not &quot;does the therapy change your regulation?&quot; (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/famp.12972" rel="noopener">baseline emotion regulation and change in relationship satisfaction among couples in EFT, Family Process, DOI 10.1111/famp.12972</a>).</p>
<p><strong>EFT for individuals (EFIT) measures symptoms, not regulation capacity.</strong> The first RCT of EFIT for depression and anxiety (2025) used the Outcome Questionnaire-30.2 and PROMIS Depression/Anxiety scales — symptom distress measures, not emotion-regulation <em>capacity</em> as a performance. The therapy improved symptoms significantly, but whether it built a <em>measurable regulation skill</em> (e.g., better performance on an emotion-regulation task, lower physiological reactivity to a stressor) was not tested (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40638305/" rel="noopener">Wiebe et al., &quot;A randomized controlled trial of emotionally focused individual therapy (EFIT) for depression and anxiety,&quot; Psychotherapy 2025, PMID 40638305</a>).</p>
<p><strong>Several EFT studies measure emotion regulation by self-report — but as a secondary outcome, never as a performance.</strong> Multiple studies report that EFT for couples improves emotion regulation measured by the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) or the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) — self-report questionnaires that ask &quot;how hard is it for you to control your emotions?&quot; not &quot;can you actually down-regulate when provoked?&quot; These measures correlate with relationship satisfaction and improve with treatment, but they are self-perception, not performance. No EFT trial has used a behavioral or physiological regulation task (e.g., affective picture viewing with instructed reappraisal, recovery from a mood induction, physiological reactivity to conflict) as the dependent variable (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=emotionally+focused+therapy+emotion+regulation+outcome" rel="noopener">EFT emotion regulation studies via PubMed</a>; <a href="https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=emotionally+focused+therapy+emotion+regulation+anger+rumination+marital+intimacy" rel="noopener">Effectiveness of EFT on emotion regulation, anger rumination, marital intimacy</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The interoception wing found the same gap from the other side.</strong> <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-responsive-other">the-responsive-other</a> and <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fading-the-other">fading-the-other</a> found that partner-based practices build interoceptive awareness — but always on self-report scales, never on objective accuracy. The pattern repeats: the relationship literature measures <em>felt</em> regulation, never <em>performed</em> regulation.</p>
<p><strong>The missing variable might hide in an existing dataset.</strong> The EFT RCTs that collected DERS or ERQ at pre and post already have the <em>self-report</em> version of the variable. The <em>performance</em> version — a regulation task administered alongside the questionnaire — is the addition that would close the gap. It would cost little to add to an existing protocol: an emotion-regulation task (instructed reappraisal during affective image viewing, or recovery from a negative mood induction, with physiological measurement) at pre and post, per partner. No located study has done this.</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> The self-report version of the variable exists in the data of several EFT trials, buried as a predictor or secondary outcome. The performance version — the one <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-unwalked-bridge">the-unwalked-bridge</a> named as the true test of Tronick's lifespan claim — does not exist in any located study. The two halves of the bridge remain: the micro-coding of the dyad's rhythm (done, in therapy and marriage research) and the measurement of the individual's own regulation as a <em>capacity that shifts</em> (done only as self-report, never as performance). The crossing is still unbuilt.</p>
<p>uncertain: &quot;no located study&quot; means not found in accessible English-language web sources; a dissertation, a small trial, or an unpublished dataset could hold the performance measure. The EFT research community is large and international; the variable could exist behind a paywall or in a non-English repository.</p>
