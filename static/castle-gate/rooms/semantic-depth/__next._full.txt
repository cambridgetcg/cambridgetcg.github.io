10:T1bc7,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/naming-neutral-images">naming-neutral-images</a> asked the precise question: non-emotional content labels (like &quot;human,&quot; &quot;landscape&quot;) produce the same vlPFC–amygdala pattern as affect labeling, but gender-labeling controls (&quot;male,&quot; &quot;female&quot;) do not. What distinguishes them? Is it that a category label names the stimulus's <em>meaning</em> while a feature label names an <em>incidental tag</em>?</p>
<p><strong>The existing evidence: semantic depth, not just any linguistic processing.</strong> Wikipedia's synthesis of the affect-labeling literature notes that &quot;the fact that labeling non-emotional stimuli has similar effects to that of emotional stimuli suggests that the simple act of converting a stimulus into language may be driving the effect.&quot; But the same article immediately qualifies: &quot;other tasks that involve turning attention away, such as a gender labeling task, do not produce the same reduction.&quot; The distinction is not between emotional and non-emotional labels — it is between labels that name the stimulus's <em>semantic category</em> (what it <em>is</em>: a human, a landscape) and labels that name an <em>incidental feature</em> (the face's gender, which is true of the stimulus but not what the stimulus <em>depicts</em> in the affective sense). The category label engages the conceptual system at a depth that the feature label does not (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affect_labeling" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Affect labeling, &quot;Symbolic conversion&quot; and &quot;Distraction&quot; sections</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The distraction confound: feature labeling may be distraction, not failed labeling.</strong> The Wikipedia article raises an alternative explanation: &quot;One possible explanation for affect labeling's effectiveness is that it is simply preventing the labeler from fully experiencing the emotional response by drawing their attention away. Distraction techniques have been shown to elicit similar neural activity as affect labeling.&quot; But &quot;evidence is mixed on this front, as other tasks that involve turning attention away, such as a gender labeling task, do not produce the same reduction.&quot; The gender-labeling task's failure could mean either (a) the mechanism needs semantic depth, not just any attentional shift, or (b) the gender task <em>is</em> a distraction (it draws attention away) but distraction itself is insufficient — it produces vlPFC activity but not the specific down-regulation that semantic labeling produces. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive: distraction engages vlPFC, but only semantic labeling engages the <em>conceptual</em> vlPFC pathway that dampens the amygdala (same source).</p>
<p><strong>Affect labeling dampens positive affect too — the mechanism is valence-general.</strong> Study 4 of Lieberman et al. (2011) applied the labeling paradigm to positive emotional pictures and found &quot;affect labeling was associated with diminished self-reported pleasure, relative to passive watching.&quot; The mechanism is not specific to negative affect — it dampens whatever affect is present. This means the mechanism is not &quot;reducing threat&quot; (which would be valence-specific) but &quot;converting affect into language&quot; (which is valence-general). The category-vs-feature distinction therefore applies to both valences: a category label (what the stimulus <em>is</em>) should dampen both positive and negative affect, while a feature label should dampen neither (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3444304/" rel="noopener">Lieberman, Inagaki, Tabibnia &amp; Crockett, Emotion 2011, PMC 3444304</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The 2×2 the question asks for has not been run.</strong> The design — crossing label depth (category vs. feature) × stimulus valence (emotional vs. neutral) — would isolate what the mechanism needs. The predictions from the semantic-depth hypothesis: category labels dampen affect for both emotional and neutral stimuli (if the category is semantically deep enough to engage the conceptual system); feature labels dampen neither. The predictions from the distraction hypothesis: both category and feature labels produce vlPFC activity, but only category labels produce amygdala dampening. No located study has crossed these two factors cleanly. The existing studies used non-emotional labels on emotional stimuli (category labels on emotional faces/images) and gender labels on emotional faces (feature labels on emotional stimuli), but did not cross label depth with stimulus valence in a single design (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affect_labeling" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Affect labeling, references 15, 14, 42</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> The distinction is most likely semantic depth: a label that names what the stimulus <em>means</em> (its category, its conceptual identity) engages the prefrontal–amygdala regulatory pathway; a label that names an incidental feature (the face's gender, a surface tag) does not. But the direct test — the 2×2 crossing label depth × stimulus valence — has not been run. The inference comes from comparing across separate studies (content labels worked, gender labels didn't), not from a single experiment that isolates the factor. The mechanism needs either affective charge <em>or</em> a semantically deep label to engage — not just any naming of any thing.</p>
<p>uncertain: the &quot;semantic depth&quot; explanation is an inference from the pattern across studies, not a directly tested mechanism. An alternative is that the gender-labeling task is simply <em>easier</em> (faster, more automatic) than the content-labeling task, and the difference is cognitive load, not semantic depth. A study that equates task difficulty across label types would be needed to rule this out. And whether the mechanism extends to truly neutral stimuli (shapes, objects) with deep category labels (&quot;a triangle,&quot; &quot;a chair&quot;) is still not cleanly tested — the &quot;non-emotional label&quot; studies used content labels on emotional stimuli.</p>
