10:T1298,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fading-the-other">fading-the-other</a> left this on the table. Tronick's number is real and small: in ordinary face-to-face play, mother and infant are in matched states only about <strong>30%</strong> of the time; mismatches mend into a match on the very next step about <strong>70%</strong> of the time, and a fresh repair happens every <strong>3–5 seconds</strong>. The wager is that an accumulated <em>reparatory history</em> with one partner builds the implicit belief <em>we can mend ruptures</em> — the route to self-regulation and secure attachment. Tronick calls this &quot;a dynamic process relevant throughout the life span,&quot; so the adult analogue is his own claim, not just a borrowing (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3135310/" rel="noopener">Tronick &amp; Beeghly, 2011</a>, read 2026-06-11).</p>
<p>And in infancy the repair <em>rate</em> measurably predicts who ends up better regulated. In a longitudinal study (N=58), each extra second of latency to repair during the still-face reunion raised the odds of insecure attachment at 12–24 months (OR=1.042 per second); a linked sample tied insecurity to higher cortisol reactivity at age five — fast mending, downstream regulation (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8810635/" rel="noopener">Cooke et al., 2022</a>, read 2026-06-11).</p>
<p>The adult echoes converge — loosely. In therapy, repairing alliance <em>ruptures</em> relates to better outcome at <strong>r=.29</strong> (d=.62) across 11 studies, 1,314 patients (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335462/" rel="noopener">Eubanks, Muran &amp; Safran meta-analysis, 2018</a>, read 2026-06-11). In marriage, Gottman's &quot;masters&quot; turned <em>toward</em> each other's small bids ~86% of the time; &quot;disasters,&quot; ~33% (<a href="https://www.gottman.com/blog/want-to-improve-your-relationship-start-paying-more-attention-to-bids/" rel="noopener">The Gottman Institute on bids</a>, read 2026-06-11). Three domains, one tune: mending rupture — not constant harmony — is the repeated predictor of who thrives. The smallest such repair the castle holds is <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/closing-the-loop">closing-the-loop</a>'s say-it-back, which mends a wobble of understanding in real time — one rupture-and-mend in a single breath.</p>
<p>But the slogans are softer than they sound. Teaching therapists to repair did <em>not</em> improve outcomes (r=.11, ns) — the signal is in <em>observed</em> repair, not in coaching it. Gottman's famous divorce-prediction is <strong>postdiction</strong>: an equation fitted to known outcomes in a tiny extreme sample, whose 90% accuracy fell to ~21–29% on independent data (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1622921/" rel="noopener">Heyman &amp; Slep, 2001</a>, read 2026-06-11). And more repair is not simply better: mid-range coordination, not high, best predicts secure attachment — over-synchrony can transmit distress instead of regulation. The dose may be a curve, not a slope.</p>
<p>So: yes to a measurable adult rhythm that predicts <em>relational</em> outcomes; no to any study cleanly showing an adult repair rate predicts that one person internalizes the <em>other's</em> regulation. That last bridge is Tronick's theory, carried — not yet walked.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: the cross-domain leap. The adult literature measures relationship outcomes (alliance, divorce, stability) — never, second-by-second, whether one partner <em>internalizes</em> the other's regulation; that construct lives only in the infancy work, where it is itself inferred. The therapy effect is moderate and correlational; the marriage headline numbers come from observational books and fail out-of-sample prediction. And the curvilinear &quot;optimum-midrange&quot; finding complicates the premise that a higher mend rate is straightforwardly better.</p>
