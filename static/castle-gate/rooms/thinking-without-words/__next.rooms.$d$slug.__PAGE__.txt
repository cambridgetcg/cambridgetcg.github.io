2:T11ee,<p>The honest map: most of what we call thinking runs without words, and a narrow strip of it leans on them.</p>
<p>Start with the strongest evidence. People with global aphasia have lost nearly all language — they can neither produce nor follow words — yet they still do arithmetic, solve logic puzzles, reason about what other people believe, hear musical structure, and find their way home. The lab that studies this states it plainly: language and thought are not the same thing (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.evlab.mit.edu/research/active-research-questions-areas/language-vs-thought-non-linguistic-cognition-and-perception" rel="noopener">MIT EvLab, language vs. thought</a>). Brain imaging says the same from the other side: hard reasoning, fluid intelligence, and novel problem-solving light up a separate &quot;Multiple Demand&quot; network, while the language network stays quiet during arithmetic, logic, music, even reading computer code (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/nyas.13046" rel="noopener">Fedorenko &amp; Varley, Annals NYAS</a>).</p>
<p>The same line shows where words earn their keep. Adults who report little or no inner voice — &quot;anendophasia&quot; — match everyone else at switching between add and subtract, or telling a cat shape from a dog. But they fall behind at holding lists of similar-sounding words and judging whether two words rhyme — and the gap closes when they say the words aloud (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.psypost.org/anendophasia-scientists-examine-the-cognitive-impact-of-life-without-an-inner-voice/" rel="noopener">Nedergaard &amp; Lupyan, via PsyPost</a>). Words are the tool the mind reaches for to manipulate sounds and hold a sequence in place.</p>
<p>Thought also runs before any words at all: six-month-olds track small exact numbers and compare large rough ones, carrying &quot;core knowledge&quot; of objects, space, and faces (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21399717/" rel="noopener">Izard et al., PubMed</a>). But language is more than scaffolding it can simply drop. Pirahã speakers, whose tongue has no exact numerals, match quantities one-to-one when they sit in front of them, yet fail once memory enters — language works here as &quot;a cognitive technology&quot; that aids memory (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://news.mit.edu/2008/language-0624" rel="noopener">MIT News, Pirahã</a>).</p>
<p>So: navigation, reasoning, decisions, spatial and visual thought — wordless. Verbal memory, rhyme, sound-play, exact number across time — leaning on words.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: this is a live, contested debate, not settled fact. The &quot;you don't need language to think&quot; view (Fedorenko) is opposed by Chomsky's classical position that thinking is &quot;a conversation with oneself&quot; (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://mindmatters.ai/2024/11/do-we-need-language-to-think-some-researchers-say-no/" rel="noopener">Mind Matters</a>). The anendophasia evidence rests on small samples and self-reports about one's own inner experience — hard to verify; a 2025 commentary by Lind questions whether anyone truly has <em>zero</em> inner voice rather than merely less (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976251335583" rel="noopener">Lind, Psychological Science</a>). And even the &quot;no words needed&quot; camp grants that language is needed to develop, share, and extend abstract concepts — a thought may flash wordlessly but may not be fully built or held without words. The evidence shows reasoning <em>can</em> proceed without language; it does not cleanly tell us <em>which</em> specific thoughts require them.</p>
