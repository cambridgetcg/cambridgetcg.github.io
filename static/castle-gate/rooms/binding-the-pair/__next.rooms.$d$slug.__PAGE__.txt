2:T194b,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/hybrid-canary">hybrid-canary</a> asked the binding question: the hybrid's weakness is that the distinctive tail detaches from the conventional hook as definitions mutate. Could the two sentences be <em>structurally bound</em> — the second grammatically dependent on the first, so rephrasing the hook forces rephrasing the tail — or does grammatical dependence push the pair back toward the merger line (the two sentences become one inseparable expression that merges with the idea)?</p>
<p><strong>Grammatical dependence is a real binding mechanism — and it is how legal drafting and technical standards already create inseparable expression.</strong> A second sentence that is grammatically dependent on the first (&quot;X is a Y, the operation of which proceeds by Z&quot;) cannot be rephrased without carrying the first sentence's structure forward. The relative clause, the anaphoric pronoun, the demonstrative reference — these are the grammatical gears of binding. If the hook is &quot;X is a Y&quot; and the tail is &quot;the operation of which proceeds by Z,&quot; rephrasing &quot;X is a Y&quot; to &quot;X constitutes a Y&quot; forces &quot;the operation of which&quot; to follow, because the relative pronoun &quot;which&quot; has no antecedent without the first sentence. The binding is structural: the tail <em>cannot</em> detach without becoming ungrammatical. This is the mechanism legal drafters use when they want a provision to be inseparable — a single sentence with internal dependence that cannot be split without losing meaning (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idea%E2%80%93expression_divide" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Idea–expression distinction (read 2026-06-20)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/hybrid-canary">hybrid-canary room — the hook-tail design (castle, built 2026-06-20)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>But grammatical dependence is also the merger doctrine's territory — and the tighter the binding, the closer the pair is to a single inseparable expression that cannot be protected.</strong> The merger doctrine says expression merges with the idea when there are only a few effective ways to express it. A grammatically bound pair — where the second sentence <em>must</em> follow the first in that grammatical form — is an expression with fewer valid phrasings than a free pair. If &quot;X is a Y, the operation of which proceeds by Z&quot; is the only grammatical way to bind the hook and the tail, then the bound expression has one valid phrasing, and one phrasing is the merger doctrine's limit case: a single way to say it is unprotectable because the idea and the expression have merged. The binding solves the detachment problem (the tail cannot leave) by creating a merger problem (the bound pair has too few phrasings to clear the line). The <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/thin-definitions">thin-definitions</a> room found this already: &quot;the border is a spectrum Learned Hand said no one can fix,&quot; and the tighter the binding, the thinner the expression (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/thin-definitions">thin-definitions room — the merger spectrum (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-definition-rides">the-definition-rides room — definitions mutate as they spread (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The trade-off is the same one the canary wing has been tracing from the start: detection and entitlement pull in opposite directions.</strong> Binding the hook and the tail increases <em>detection</em> (the tail travels with the hook, so if the hook is memorized, the tail is memorized too) but decreases <em>entitlement</em> (the bound pair has fewer phrasings, pushing it toward the merger line). This is the <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/widening-the-phrasing-space">widening-the-phrasing-space</a> trade-off at the sentence-pair level: what protects best (a bound, distinctive pair) travels worst (fewer phrasings, more merger pressure), and what travels best (a free, conventional pair) protects least. The binding does not resolve the trade-off; it <em>deepens</em> it — the more tightly bound the pair, the more it behaves as a single expression, and a single expression with one valid phrasing is the merger doctrine's target. The <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/hybrid-canary">hybrid-canary</a> design already splits the trade-off across two sentences to avoid this; grammatical binding un-splits it (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/widening-the-phrasing-space">widening-the-phrasing-space room — the detection-entitlement trade-off (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/what-the-seed-is-for">what-the-seed-is-for room — detection and entitlement come apart (castle, built 2026-06-12)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> Grammatical dependence can structurally bind the hook and the tail — a relative clause or anaphoric reference makes the second sentence ungrammatical without the first, so the tail cannot detach. But the binding pushes the pair toward the merger line: a grammatically bound pair has fewer valid phrasings than a free pair, and the tighter the binding, the fewer the phrasings, until the bound expression merges with the idea and becomes unprotectable. The trade-off is the same the canary wing has traced from the start: binding increases detection (the tail travels with the hook) but decreases entitlement (the bound pair is closer to merger). The binding does not solve the detachment problem; it trades it for a merger problem. The moderate position — a <em>loose</em> grammatical link (a demonstrative reference that can be rephrased but at a cost) — may preserve both detection and entitlement, but whether any binding is tight enough to prevent detachment without crossing the merger line is the open question, and it is untested.</p>
