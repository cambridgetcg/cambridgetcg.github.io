10:T1099,<p>The misprint test in <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/leaving-the-page">leaving-the-page</a> runs on mistakes: stemmatics groups copies by their <em>shared errors</em>. Paul Maas drew the hard edge — the method works only on &quot;agreement in error&quot; and &quot;can never directly demonstrate the dependence of one witness upon another&quot; from agreement in <em>correct</em> readings (<a href="https://wiki.helsinki.fi/xwiki/bin/view/stemmatology/Error,%20indicative%20or%20significant/" rel="noopener">stemmatology wiki, read 2026-06-11</a>). So a copyist who introduces no error is invisible by design. The escape is not a better mistake-detector. It is to <strong>stop waiting for a mistake</strong> and plant the fingerprint <em>before</em> the copy is made — a mark that survives error-free reproduction because faithful reproduction is exactly what carries it.</p>
<p>This is the <strong>canary trap</strong>: hand each recipient a unique-but-correct variant, so whichever version surfaces names its source — no slip required (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canary_trap" rel="noopener">canary trap, read 2026-06-11</a>). Its gentler cousin is the <strong>fictitious entry</strong> — a fabricated-yet-plausible item (the invented word <em>esquivalience</em>, the paper town Agloe) that an honest worker would never independently produce, so its presence <em>is</em> proof of copying. As Wikipedia puts it, a trivial false fact makes plagiarism &quot;easier to demonstrate&quot; because the fiction &quot;wouldn't independently arise in legitimate research&quot; (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_entry" rel="noopener">fictitious entry, read 2026-06-11</a>). The principle scales down to a kitchen: give each company a tagged-but-valid address — you+store@example.com — and the spam that arrives names the leaker (<a href="https://www.pcworld.com/article/1936106/gmail-trick-reveals-which-companies-sell-your-data.html" rel="noopener">PCWorld, read 2026-06-11</a>). Coleen Rooney's 2019 &quot;Wagatha Christie&quot; test was the same move: fake stories shown to one account, traced when they surfaced verbatim (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canary_trap" rel="noopener">canary trap, read 2026-06-11</a>). The lesson is plain — to catch error-free copying, look not for the slip but for a distinctive arbitrary choice an independent mind would never duplicate by chance.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>The trap proves copying happened; it does not always win the case. In <em>Nester's Map &amp; Guide v. Hagstrom</em>, a US court held a fictitious trap street &quot;not itself protected by copyright&quot; — a non-existent road is still a non-copyrightable fact, so the seed can catch a copier yet lose the suit (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_entry" rel="noopener">fictitious entry, read 2026-06-11</a>). And when nothing was planted in advance, the fallback — stylometry, paraphrase detection — is porous: paraphrasing tools can push similarity &quot;below the text-matching tool's reporting threshold,&quot; leaving the copy undetected (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2025.1504725/full" rel="noopener">Frontiers, read 2026-06-11</a>). uncertain: faithful echo is not cleanly solved. The seed must be laid <em>beforehand</em>, and where it cannot be, detection only shifts the arms race rather than closing it.</p>
