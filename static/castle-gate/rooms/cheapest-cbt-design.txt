10:T10a1,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/cbt-openness-trait">cbt-openness-trait</a> asked for the cheapest design: a standard CBT protocol with a Big Five inventory at pre, post, and follow-up. The surprise: someone has already run nearly this exact study, and it returned a positive answer — openness moved.</p>
<p><strong>The study that already crossed.</strong> Kennair, Solem, Hagen, Havnen, Nysaeter &amp; Hjemdal (2021) ran a randomized controlled trial of CBT (n = 28) versus metacognitive therapy (MCT, n = 32) for generalized anxiety disorder, administering the full 240-item Revised NEO Personality Inventory (NEO-PI-R) before and after 12 sessions. The result: treatment across conditions was associated with a significant reduction in Neuroticism and a significant <em>increase in Extraversion and Openness</em>. Agreeableness and Conscientiousness did not change. The authors call this &quot;the first study to show that efficient treatment for a specific disorder resulted in changes across NEO-PI-R factors and facets&quot; (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33338315/" rel="noopener">Kennair et al., Clinical Psychology &amp; Psychotherapy 2021, DOI 10.1002/cpp.2541</a>).</p>
<p><strong>What the existing study tells us about detectability.</strong> The d = .37 from Roberts et al. is the <em>across-therapy, across-trait</em> average — emotional stability moves most, openness least. The Kennair study found openness moved significantly even in a small sample (n = 55 completers), suggesting the effect is not vanishingly small: GAD patients enter with <em>lower</em> openness than normals, and treatment partly restores it. The trait change is associated with symptom improvement, but the direction of causality is not established — it could be that feeling less anxious makes you more open, or that becoming more open makes you less anxious (same source).</p>
<p><strong>The cheapest clean design is therefore not a new trial but a reanalysis or an add-on.</strong> The design the question asked for — &quot;a standard CBT protocol with a Big Five inventory at pre, post, and follow-up&quot; — is exactly what Kennair et al. ran, minus the follow-up. Adding a 3- or 6-month follow-up NEO-PI-R to the next standard CBT RCT would cost the price of the questionnaire and its scoring — no new arms, no new therapy. The effect should be detectable: Kennair found it at n = 55, so a powered study (n = 100+) with follow-up would have the sensitivity to ask whether the gain holds or decays (as the library study's did at two years).</p>
<p><strong>The honest limit.</strong> The Kennair study measured trait change as a <em>byproduct of symptom treatment</em>, not as a <em>target</em>. The question the room was asked from — whether CBT's appraisal mechanism specifically moves openness — is not answered by showing that openness rose alongside anxiety falling. The <em>mechanism</em> question needs an intervention that trains open appraisals directly (the second door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/cbt-openness-trait">cbt-openness-trait</a>), not one that happens to move openness as a side effect. The cheapest <em>descriptive</em> design has been run; the cheapest <em>mechanism-isolating</em> design has not.</p>
<p>uncertain: the Kennair study did not report openness-specific effect sizes (only significance), so the exact d for openness change cannot be compared to the d = .37 baseline from here. And the GAD population's lower baseline openness means the change may be a <em>normalization</em> (return to population mean) rather than a true trait shift — the design cannot distinguish the two.</p>

A mediation design that measures both appraisal change (open interpretations of situations) and symptom change would ask which carries which.</li><li>The GAD population starts with lower openness — is the rise a normalization to the population mean or a true trait shift past it?
