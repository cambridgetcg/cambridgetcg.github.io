2:Tef6,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/appraisal-engine">appraisal-engine</a> named a clean gap: CBT trains appraisals directly, the Roberts meta-analysis found therapy moves traits, but no study has checked whether CBT specifically shifts openness. This room walks through it and finds the gap confirmed — not because the measurement was run and openness stayed flat, but because the two literatures have never been joined at the seam where they would meet.</p>
<p>The strongest synthesis is Roberts et al.'s systematic review of 207 intervention studies, which found that therapy <em>does</em> change personality traits — d = .37 over roughly 24 weeks — with emotional stability moving most, then extraversion; openness was not the primary mover. Critically, the type of therapy (CBT, psychodynamic, eclectic) was &quot;not strongly associated with the amount of change&quot; (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28054797/" rel="noopener">Roberts et al., Psychological Bulletin 2017</a>). So CBT was represented in the meta-analysis, and it moved traits at the same modest rate as everything else — but the synthesis tracked <em>trait change as a byproduct of therapy</em>, not <em>CBT's appraisal mechanism tested against openness as a target</em>. The studies inside it measured symptoms — depression, anxiety — and some happened to also carry Big Five data, but the analysis asked &quot;does therapy change traits,&quot; not &quot;does CBT's appraisal training specifically move openness.&quot;</p>
<p>The interventions that <em>did</em> move openness as a target went through action, not appraisal. PEACH's if-then action plans and Hudson's trait-typical challenges both worked through behavior, with volitional acceptance as the gateway (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7923371/" rel="noopener">Stieger et al., PEACH, PMC</a>; <a href="https://www.nathanwhudson.com/vita/pdf/Hudson%20et%20al.,%202019a.pdf" rel="noopener">Hudson et al. 2019</a>). And the library study's openness gain did not survive two years (read 2026-06-18 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26998917/" rel="noopener">Sander et al. 2017</a>). So the personality-change literature and the CBT-outcome literature exist side by side — one measures symptoms, the other measures traits — and the bridge between them, where a CBT trial pre-posts Big Five openness and asks whether the appraisal mechanism specifically moves it, is unbuilt.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: the Roberts meta-analysis did include CBT studies and did report trait change, so it is possible that openness moved <em>somewhat</em> within CBT arms and the synthesis simply did not isolate it — the data may exist, buried in a subgroup no one extracted. The honest claim is that no <em>located</em> study has asked the mechanism question cleanly, not that the data cannot exist. And the &quot;therapy type doesn't matter&quot; finding may not generalize to appraisal-training <em>designed for trait growth</em> rather than for symptom relief — CBT for depression is not CBT for openness, even if both train appraisals.</p>
