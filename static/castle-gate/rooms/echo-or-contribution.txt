10:T1422,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/reading-a-literature">reading-a-literature</a> left the reader counting independent signals rather than papers. This room asks the page-level question: held in your hand, which paper is a signal and which is the room ringing?</p>
<p>The first mark is mechanical and decisive: <strong>does the paper carry primary data?</strong> Greenberg traced an entire belief network — 242 papers, every citation path — and found authority grew by <em>amplification</em>: papers that present no data yet get cited as if they settled the claim. Only 4 of the 10 most authoritative papers held experimental data; one data-free review carried 63% of all citation paths (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2714656/" rel="noopener">Greenberg, read 2026-06-11</a>). A data-free paper, however eminent, is structurally an echo.</p>
<p>The second mark lives in <strong>how it cites</strong>. An echo and a contribution use the prior literature differently, and the difference shows on the page. Greenberg catalogued the tells: citation bias (supportive primary data got 94% of citations, contrary data 6%), <em>transmutation</em> (a hypothesis cited as established fact), <em>diversion</em> (citing a source for what it does not say), and dead-end citations to papers holding no relevant statement. So trace the key citations: if they reach original measurements that bear the claim, the paper contributes; if they loop into data-free reviews or misstate the source, it restates the shared prior. This check earns its keep — across studies, between 1 in 5 and 1 in 10 citations are quotation errors. The opioid letter is the parable: a 1980 five-sentence note about <em>hospitalized</em> patients, cited 600+ times as proof opioids rarely addict, its scope dropped by 80% of citers (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8054004/" rel="noopener">Quotation errors, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<p>The third mark is <strong>direction</strong>. In forecasting, the analog of new-signal-vs-echo is <em>bold</em> versus <em>herding</em>: a revision is bold when it moves away from both the forecaster's own prior and the consensus. Bold forecasts are more accurate, and boldness rises with the analyst's past accuracy (<a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2005.00731.x" rel="noopener">Clement &amp; Tse, read 2026-06-11</a>). A source that departs from consensus — against its own earlier position — more likely carries a private signal than one converging onto the crowd.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: every mark here is <em>probabilistic, not proof</em>, and the premise itself may be too generous to the reader. Shared and private components cannot be separated from estimates alone; even a quantitative method needs extra elicitation to pull them apart, so a reader eyeing one paper is unlikely to do it reliably (<a href="https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3047" rel="noopener">Palley &amp; Soll / shared-info, read 2026-06-11</a>). Novelty does not equal independence: a paper can genuinely add content and still be statistically dependent, sharing systematic error modes with its base prior (<a href="https://eepower.com/technical-articles/on-chip-redundancy-and-the-myth-of-independence/" rel="noopener">redundancy myth, read 2026-06-11</a>). And the most visible mark of all — <em>self-declared</em> novelty — is inflated and gameable: promotional terms in abstracts rose almost ninefold from 1974 to 2014, while even formal novelty indicators struggle to tell novel from non-novel articles apart (<a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17456" rel="noopener">novelty measures, read 2026-06-11</a>). The citation count, the obvious shortcut, is itself an echo signal — driven by herding and status more than rigour (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/research-metrics-and-analytics/articles/10.3389/frma.2016.00001/full" rel="noopener">citation critique, read 2026-06-11</a>). The most reliable move is not a mark on the page at all but to <em>leave</em> it: follow the footnotes back until they terminate in a primary source or dead-end in a loop — circular reporting wearing many coats (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_reporting" rel="noopener">circular reporting, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
