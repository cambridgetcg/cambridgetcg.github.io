2:Tc51,<p>What gathers here: why some explanations feel simple, and why that feeling is both a tool and a trap.</p>
<p>An explanation feels simple when the mind processes it easily. Psychologists call this ease <a href="/castle-gate/words/fluency">fluency</a> — and the feeling is not produced by the ideas alone. Anything that smooths processing makes the <em>content</em> feel simpler and truer: rhyming sayings are believed more than the same sayings unrhymed (&quot;woes unite foes&quot; beats &quot;woes unite enemies&quot;), and in the weeks after companies go public, stocks with easy-to-pronounce names outperform hard-to-pronounce ones — the ease of saying the name is mistaken for the quality of the thing. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://people.uncw.edu/tothj/Extra-Credit-Papers/Oppenheimer-The%20Secret%20Life%20of%20Fluency-TICS-2008.pdf" rel="noopener">Oppenheimer, &quot;The secret life of fluency&quot;, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 2008</a>; <a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0601071103" rel="noopener">Alter &amp; Oppenheimer, fluency and stock prices, PNAS 2006</a>.)</p>
<p>So the honest answer to &quot;what makes an explanation feel simple&quot; is: familiar words, short clear sentences, a shape the reader already half-knows — everything that lets the next sentence land without strain. This is why the castle's house style works: plain words <em>are</em> fluent words. And it connects to <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/linking-thoughts">linking-thoughts</a> — an idea that touches what you already know is processed easily, because the old knowledge does half the work.</p>
<p>Two warnings keep this true:</p>
<ul><li><strong>Feeling simple is not being true.</strong> The mind uses ease as a shortcut for truth, so a fluent lie can feel more right than a clumsy fact. Fluent explanations must still name their sources. (Same Oppenheimer 2008 review, read 2026-06-10.) The same ease has a second reading: the mind also feels it as beauty — this room and <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/beauty-as-fluency">beauty-as-fluency</a> are two faces of one mechanism.</li><li><strong>Sometimes friction helps.</strong> When a problem was printed in a hard-to-read font, people solved tricky reasoning questions <em>more</em> often — the difficulty switched off autopilot and switched on careful thinking. Smoothness invites trust; roughness invites checking. (Read 2026-06-10: <a href="https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~aalter/intuitive.pdf" rel="noopener">Alter, Oppenheimer, Epley &amp; Eyre, &quot;Overcoming intuition&quot;, 2007</a>.) Out in the world, the same quiet tax decides which habits live: <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/friction-decides">friction-decides</a> is this lever seen from the other side.</li></ul>
