10:T10d6,<p>What gathers here: the marriage half of <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/echo-between-equals">echo-between-equals</a>. Couples therapy stages the echo as the <em>speaker–listener technique</em> — one partner holds the floor, the other paraphrases before answering (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1066480797051013" rel="noopener">Stanley, Markman &amp; Blumberg, <em>The Speaker/Listener Technique</em>, 1997</a>, read 2026-06-10). Does it survive real anger?</p>
<p>Inside real anger, no. The two warring camps agree on this single fact. John Gottman's side brings physiology: past roughly 100 heartbeats a minute — <em>flooding</em> — the capacity to listen, take perspective and process nuance goes offline, and only a 20–30 minute break restores it (<a href="https://www.gottman.com/blog/manage-conflict-part-4/" rel="noopener">The Gottman Institute, <em>Manage Conflict — Part 4</em></a>, read 2026-06-10). His coders, watching happy stable couples quarrel for years, could not find paraphrase-and-validate exchanges at all — &quot;we haven't found any examples&quot; — and the Munich study found couples intensively trained in active listening mostly still distressed, the improvers relapsed within months (<a href="https://www.psychotherapy.net/interview/couples/john-gottman" rel="noopener">Gottman interview, Psychotherapy.net</a>, read 2026-06-10; <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/353438" rel="noopener">Gottman, Coan, Carrère &amp; Swanson, 1998</a>, read 2026-06-10 — no support for the active-listening model).</p>
<p>The technique's defenders answer: that misses what the script is <em>for</em>. The 1998 study was correlational, not a test of the intervention, and nobody claimed happy couples do this naturally (<a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227761409_Structural_Flaws_in_the_Bridge_From_Basic_Research_on_Marriage_to_Interventions_for_Couples" rel="noopener">Stanley, Bradbury &amp; Markman, <em>Structural Flaws in the Bridge</em>, 2000</a>; <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-6427.00232" rel="noopener">Hafen &amp; Crane, <em>the active listening controversy</em>, 2003</a>, both read 2026-06-10). The script is artificial on purpose — a brake, not a transcript: its mid-quarrel job is slowing the exchange before the flood, and its own rules say stop and cool off once flooding starts.</p>
<p>So the honest verdict: the echo does not survive <em>inside</em> anger — nothing verbal does, on either camp's evidence. It survives <em>around</em> anger: pulled early as a brake, or after the twenty quiet minutes, as <a href="/castle-gate/words/repair">repair</a> of what the storm tore. And two doubts go deeper. Newlyweds assigned to watch and discuss five relationship movies halved their divorce rate exactly as well as the skills programs did — 24% down to 11% over three years — so the script may not be the active ingredient at all (<a href="https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/divorce-rate-cut-in-half-for-couples-who-discussed-relationship-movies/" rel="noopener">University of Rochester, on Rogge et al. 2013</a>, read 2026-06-10). And Gottman's plainest line: an echo that changes nothing soothes nothing — &quot;I still haven't balanced the damned checkbook.&quot; In a quarrel, the message is often not information but a demand; <a href="/castle-gate/words/teach-back">teach-back</a> rebuilds meaning, and meaning was never the whole of what was asked.</p>
<p>uncertain: whether couples actually deploy the technique at home, unobserved and mid-quarrel, no study here measured; PREP's longer-term gains are mixed across trials and the fading is honestly contested.</p>
