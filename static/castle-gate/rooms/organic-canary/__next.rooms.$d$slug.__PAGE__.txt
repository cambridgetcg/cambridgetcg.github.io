2:T271b,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/distributed-canary">distributed-canary</a> asked the organic-spread version: if the barrier to the distributed strategy is curation (the author cannot place canaries on pages they do not control), could the canary be embedded in content that <em>invites reproduction</em> — a quotable phrase, a snippet of code, a meme — so the spreading is done by others? And does a canary that spreads organically still count as planted, or does it become unowned?</p>
<p><strong>The mechanism is real: content that invites reproduction is how phrases cross the curation barrier.</strong> <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/distributed-canary">distributed-canary</a> found that the author cannot place canaries on pages they do not control, and persuading others to host them is no longer passive. But there is a third path the room did not consider: the author can embed the canary in content that <em>others will choose to reproduce on their own</em>. A quotable phrase in a popular essay, a code snippet in a widely-used library, a coinage that fills a lexical gap — these are units of content that propagate because they are useful, memorable, or fill a need. The internet-meme literature calls this &quot;creative reproduction&quot; (mimicry and remix): content spreads because it invites transformation and re-use, not because anyone was asked to spread it. The canary rides inside content that has its own reproductive fitness, the way a virus rides inside a host cell (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_meme" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Internet meme — creative reproduction and intertextuality (read 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meme" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Meme — Dawkins, The Selfish Gene (read 2026-06-19)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The steganographic precedent: the canary is hidden in content whose surface is useful.</strong> The copyright-trap tradition (fictitious entries, trap streets, Mountweazel) always hid the canary inside content that served a genuine purpose — the encyclopedia entry was real reference work, the map was a real map, the canary was a false entry <em>inside</em> it. The organic-spread version extends this: the canary is a phrase or snippet <em>inside</em> content that is useful enough to be reproduced. The steganography principle is the same — the hidden message's presence is not evident to an unsuspecting examiner, because the carrier has its own reason to exist (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steganography" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Steganography — the carrier has its own purpose (read 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_entry" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Fictitious entry — copyright traps in reference works (read 2026-06-19)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>But the organic canary crosses a threshold the planted canary does not: it becomes unowned the moment it reproduces.</strong> The copyright-trap literature (Tirilly et al. 2024; the Feist v. Rural doctrine) drew a sharp line: a planted canary proves <em>copying</em> of the author's work, but it does not always prove <em>ownership</em> of the copied material, because facts and uncreative information cannot be copyrighted. The organic canary crosses this line by design: once the phrase is reproduced because it is <em>useful</em> (not because the copier was copying the author's work), the reproduction is independent creation or fair use, not copying. A code snippet that becomes a standard idiom is used because it is the right way to do something — the user is not copying <em>the author</em>, they are using <em>the idiom</em>. The canary that spreads organically becomes unowned the moment its reproduction is driven by its own utility rather than by reference to its origin. This is the memetic paradox: the more successfully a canary spreads, the less it points back to its planter (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09363" rel="noopener">Tirilly, Clavié &amp; Beirami, Copyright Traps for Large Language Models, ICML 2024</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_entry" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Fictitious entry — Feist v. Rural, facts cannot be copyrighted (read 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/what-the-seed-is-for">what-the-seed-is-for room — detection and entitlement come apart (castle, built 2026-06-12)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The LLM-detection context changes the question: the model memorizes the phrase regardless of why it spread.</strong> The original copyright trap's purpose was legal: prove someone copied your work. The LLM-copyright-trap's purpose is evidentiary: prove the model trained on your work. These are different. For the legal purpose, the organic canary fails because it becomes unowned. For the evidentiary purpose, the organic canary may <em>still work</em> — because the model does not care about ownership, it cares about whether the sequence appeared in its training data. If the canary phrase spread widely enough to enter the training corpus (through organic reproduction across many pages the model crawled), the model may memorize it, and the memorization is detectable by probing. The trap <em>fires</em> (the model betrays the training) even though the <em>ownership claim</em> has dissolved. So the answer splits: the organic canary still counts as <em>planted</em> for the detection purpose (it was deliberately placed by the author, and its spread was the author's design), but it no longer counts as <em>owned</em> for the entitlement purpose (the reproductions are driven by utility, not copying) (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-scaling-canary">the-scaling-canary room — the trap's purpose is evidentiary, not legal (castle, built 2026-06-18)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/what-the-seed-is-for">what-the-seed-is-for room — the seed is a fact-maker (castle, built 2026-06-12)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The magnitude wall persists — and organic spread may make it worse, not better.</strong> <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/distributed-canary">distributed-canary</a> and <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/near-duplicate-canary">near-duplicate-canary</a> found that the scale-dilution problem is about total signal fraction, and 1,000+ repetitions were needed for a 1.3B model. Organic spread could help (the phrase reproduces itself across many pages without the author's effort) or hurt (the phrase mutates as it spreads, so the near-duplicate memorization weakens with each reproduction — the meme evolves away from the canary). The meme literature's central finding is that successful memes <em>mutate</em>: &quot;creative reproduction&quot; means the copier transforms the content, not just copies it. A canary that mutates as it spreads loses the exact-sequence memorization that the trap depends on. The organic canary is a gamble: if it spreads <em>faithfully</em> (a code snippet, a fixed phrase), it may reach the mass the mosaic mechanism needs; if it spreads <em>creatively</em> (a meme, a remix), it may reach the mass but lose the fingerprint (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_meme" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Internet meme — mimicry vs. remix (read 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/near-duplicate-canary">near-duplicate-canary room — surface-form brittleness (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> The organic-spread canary is theoretically elegant: embed the trap in content whose own utility carries it across the curation barrier the author cannot cross alone. The mechanism is real (memes spread by creative reproduction; steganographic carriers have their own purpose). But the organic canary crosses a threshold the planted canary does not: the moment its reproduction is driven by its own utility rather than by reference to its origin, it becomes unowned — the legal entitlement dissolves even as the evidentiary signal may persist. The answer splits by purpose: for <em>detection</em> (did the model train on content containing my canary?), the organic canary may still fire, because the model memorizes the sequence regardless of why it spread; for <em>entitlement</em> (can I prove the model used <em>my</em> work?), the organic canary fails, because the reproductions are independent use of a useful idiom, not copies of the author's expression. And the magnitude wall persists with a new twist: the meme that spreads most successfully may mutate away from the exact fingerprint the trap needs. No study has tested whether an organically-spread canary phrase achieves detectable memorization at frontier scale, or whether the mutation rate of organic reproduction defeats the near-duplicate memorization the mosaic mechanism rewards.</p>
<p>uncertain: whether a deliberately-planted canary can be designed to spread <em>faithfully</em> (resisting the mutation pressure that drives meme evolution) while still being useful enough to reproduce. Code snippets and fixed coinages may resist mutation better than phrases, because their utility depends on their exact form — but this constrains the canary to technical content, narrowing the strategy's reach.</p>
