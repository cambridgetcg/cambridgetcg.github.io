2:T12ee,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/no-neighbors">no-neighbors</a> left the frontier, where the crowd is silent. This room walks back into the populated web and finds a stranger trap: the crowd is loud, confident — and wrong. A finding everyone cites but no lab can repeat. Here lateral reading, which judges a source by what others say of it, inherits the crowd's stale verdict and hands it back as truth.</p>
<p>In this exact regime, the reputational heuristic fails three ways. It suppresses its own correction: trust a source and only 6.7% of readers check it laterally, versus 20% for an unknown one — <em>the more respected, the less examined</em> (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8981191/" rel="noopener">PMC8981191, read 2026-06-11</a>). Prestige does not track method — statistical power runs <em>negatively</em> with journal rank (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5826185/" rel="noopener">Brembs, read 2026-06-11</a>). And it points backward: papers that fail to replicate are cited some 153 times <em>more</em> than those that hold (<a href="https://today.ucsd.edu/story/a-new-replication-crisis-research-that-is-less-likely-be-true-is-cited-more" rel="noopener">Serra-Garcia &amp; Gneezy, read 2026-06-11</a>) — the same loud-but-wrong consensus <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/reading-a-literature">reading-a-literature</a> meets on the frontier, where each citing paper raises belief in a false claim, and a retracted paper keeps drawing positive citations long after the record is fixed (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9728909/" rel="noopener">PMC9728909, read 2026-06-11</a>). What does catch the rot is internal: among the most-cited clinical studies, sample size — not citation count — predicted which findings survived (<a href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/1731778" rel="noopener">Ioannidis, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<p>So the answer is yes — but bounded. The reach claimed by &quot;even on the populated web&quot; overstates it. On the <em>ordinary</em> web, the same Stanford work that crowned lateral reading shows internal reading is where errors concentrate: historians reading a page on its merits were fooled by logos and .org domains, slower and wronger than fact-checkers who simply left (<a href="https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/10/fact-checkers-outperform-historians-evaluating-online-information" rel="noopener">Stanford, read 2026-06-11</a>). Internal auditing wins <em>in the reputable-but-unreplicable pocket</em>; lateral reading wins almost everywhere else. They are complementary, not ranked: screen the source laterally first, then audit any high-stakes claim from even a trusted name — design, sample, effect size, who paid (<a href="https://examine.com/guides/how-to-read-a-study/" rel="noopener">Examine, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: no study runs the exact head-to-head the question asks. Two honest counterweights also cut at it. First, auditing has a ceiling — the <em>inescapability of epistemic dependence</em> means even experts must defer to other experts to vet a specialized claim, so &quot;internal vs. reputation&quot; is partly a false clean line (<a href="https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/15460/1/Almassi_Ben-Experts_Evidence_Epistemic.pdf" rel="noopener">Almassi, read 2026-06-11</a>). Second, what actually corrected the record was itself a social act — a replication by <em>other</em> labs (<a href="https://today.ucsd.edu/story/a-new-replication-crisis-research-that-is-less-likely-be-true-is-cited-more" rel="noopener">UC San Diego, read 2026-06-11</a>) — not a lone reader's scrutiny. And in the wild, most people do not verify at all, staying on the results page; effective auditing needed heavy scaffolding (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8012470/" rel="noopener">PMC8012470, read 2026-06-11</a>). The realistic contest is often cheap reputation versus no check at all.</p>
