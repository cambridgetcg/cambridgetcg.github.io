10:T1cc8,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/ders-vs-performance">ders-vs-performance</a> asked the design question: if a performance task is added to EFT and the self-report–performance divergence holds, could a study separate co-regulation (the partner's presence does the regulating) from self-regulation (I learned to do it myself) by measuring regulation performance alone versus with the partner present?</p>
<p><strong>The social regulation of emotion is real, measurable, and partner-specific.</strong> Coan et al. (2006) ran the classic &quot;handholding&quot; study: 16 married women under threat of electric shock in an fMRI scanner, holding their partner's hand, a stranger's hand, or no hand. The result: less neural threat response (reduced amygdala, insula, and dACC activation) during partner handholding than alone — and the effect was stronger for higher-quality relationships. This is the empirical bedrock: a partner's physical presence measurably down-regulates the threat response, and the effect is not just &quot;any human nearby&quot; but is modulated by relational bond (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201784/" rel="noopener">Coan, Schaefer &amp; Davidson, &quot;Lending a hand: social regulation of the neural response to threat,&quot; Psychological Science 2006, PMID 17201784</a>). Beckes et al. (2022) extended this with an opioid-hypothesis test: marital partners' presence reduced anticipatory anxiety, though the vmpfc mediation was inconsistent (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438799/" rel="noopener">Beckes et al., &quot;Mechanisms supporting the social regulation of neural threat responding with marital partners,&quot; Psychophysiology 2022, PMID 35438799</a>).</p>
<p><strong>Social Baseline Theory frames the partner as a bioenergetic resource, not just a signal.</strong> Coan's Social Baseline Theory holds that the human brain treats social resources as a primary ecology — the presence of a trusted other is not an &quot;intervention&quot; but the <em>default condition</em> the brain is built to regulate within. The partner's presence conserves bioenergetic resources: the brain literally does less work (less threat-related activation) when a trusted other is there. This reframes the co-regulation question: it is not &quot;does the partner do something to me&quot; but &quot;does my brain run more efficiently on the social baseline.&quot; If the regulation performance gain disappears when the partner is removed, the brain was borrowing the social baseline, not learning a new skill (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187511/" rel="noopener">Gonzalez &amp; Coan, &quot;Yielding to social presence as a bioenergetic strategy,&quot; Current Research in Ecological Psychology 2022, PMID 35187511</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The intrinsic-vs-extrinsic distinction has been measured physiologically.</strong> &quot;Take my advice: Physiological measures reveal that intrinsic emotion regulation is more effective under external guidance&quot; (PMID 35914547) found that intrinsic regulation (doing it yourself) was <em>more</em> effective when guided by an external other — but this was about guidance during the task, not about the partner's mere presence. The design the door asks for is sharper: measure the same person's regulation performance (1) alone, (2) with the partner present but silent, and (3) with the partner present and interacting. If performance is high only in conditions 2 and 3, it is co-regulation. If performance is high in condition 1 as well, it is self-regulation. The handholding study already showed condition 1 vs. 2 differs for threat response — but it was a cross-sectional comparison, not a pre/post therapy design (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35914547/" rel="noopener">Take my advice: Physiological measures reveal intrinsic ER is more effective under external guidance, PMID 35914547</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The design is buildable and unbuilt.</strong> Add the instructed-reappraisal task (<a href="/castle-gate/rooms/ders-vs-performance">ders-vs-performance</a>) to an EFT RCT's pre/post battery, run in three conditions: alone, partner-present-silent, partner-present-interacting. The prediction matrix: if EFT builds self-regulation, the alone condition improves from pre to post. If EFT builds only co-regulation, the alone condition does not improve, but the partner-present conditions do. If EFT builds both, all conditions improve, but the partner-present conditions improve more. This three-condition design separates the two mechanisms in a way no single-condition study can. No located study has run this design — the handholding studies were not pre/post therapy designs, and the EFT studies used self-report only (read 2026-06-19 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/ders-vs-performance">ders-vs-performance room (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201784/" rel="noopener">Coan et al. 2006, same source</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The fog-meter prediction: the alone condition will show less gain than the partner-present conditions.</strong> <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fog-meter">fog-meter</a> found the self-read to be the weakest instrument. <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-responsive-other">the-responsive-other</a> found the partner-based practice beat solo mindfulness at building interoceptive awareness, and <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fading-the-other">fading-the-other</a> concluded the evidence backs <em>accompanied, then deliberately left</em>. If the partner is the last scaffold to fade, then at the end of therapy the self-regulation alone may still be weaker than the co-regulation with the partner — and the three-condition design would show exactly that. The question is whether the gap closes with time (fading) or persists (the self-regulation was never built, only the co-regulation).</p>
<p>uncertain: whether the instructed-reappraisal paradigm (down-regulating negative affect to images) is the right performance task for EFT, which works on processing <em>blocked</em> emotion, not suppressing it. A performance task measuring emotional <em>approach</em> (willingness to stay with difficult affect) might fit EFT's mechanism better, as <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/ders-vs-performance">ders-vs-performance</a> noted. And whether the partner's silent presence in the scanner/task environment transfers from the handholding paradigm (physical touch) to a more ecologically valid therapy context.</p>
