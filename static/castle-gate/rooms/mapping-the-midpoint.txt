10:T18b7,<p>The door from <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/widening-the-phrasing-space">widening-the-phrasing-space</a> asked the empirical question: the canary-author's craft is to find the &quot;moderate unconventionality&quot; midpoint — a definition distinctive enough to clear the merger line but conventional enough to be copied verbatim. Can this midpoint be identified in advance, or only discovered after the fact? And could a corpus study of real coined terms map where it sits?</p>
<p><strong>Corpus linguistics has the tools to track how a term's first definition survives or mutates as it spreads — but the study has not been run for this purpose.</strong> Historical corpora (Google Books Ngram, the Corpus of Historical American English, the Oxford English Dictionary's quotation database) can trace when a term first appeared and how its definition was phrased across decades. Semantic-change research uses these corpora to study how word meanings drift over time (<a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-definition-rides">the-definition-rides</a> drew on this literature for its &quot;definitions mutate&quot; finding). The method is straightforward: identify a set of coined technical terms, find their first published definition, track every subsequent definition in the corpus, and code whether the later definitions reproduce the first verbatim, paraphrase it, or replace it entirely. The midpoint — the level of unconventionality at which definitions are most often reproduced verbatim — is the empirical target. The tool exists; the study does not (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_change" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Semantic change — historical corpora (read 2026-06-20)</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_linguistics" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Corpus linguistics (read 2026-06-20)</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Books_Ngram_Viewer" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Google Books Ngram Viewer (read 2026-06-20)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The &quot;in advance&quot; question is harder, because the midpoint depends on the adoption community, which the author does not control.</strong> A definition's verbatim reproduction depends on whether the adopting community finds it <em>good enough to use as-is</em> or <em>in need of rephrasing for their context</em>. <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/widening-the-phrasing-space">widening-the-phrasing-space</a> found that unconventional definitions are rephrased by adopters who do not share the cross-field analogy, while conventional definitions are copied because they fit the genre. The midpoint sits where the definition is distinctive enough to be <em>recognizable</em> (the author can claim it) but conventional enough to be <em>useful</em> (adopters have no reason to rephrase). But &quot;useful&quot; depends on the community: a definition that is conventional for software engineers may be unconventional for biologists, and the author cannot know in advance which community will adopt the term. The midpoint is not a property of the definition alone; it is a property of the definition <em>in relation to its adoption community</em>, which the author may not know at the moment of writing (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/widening-the-phrasing-space">widening-the-phrasing-space room — the detection-entitlement trade-off (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/the-definition-rides">the-definition-rides room — definitions mutate as they spread (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The corpus study could map the midpoint empirically — but it would be descriptive, not predictive, because the adoption community is a moving target.</strong> A study of, say, 100 coined technical terms (from computer science, bioinformatics, or another young field) could code each term's first definition for unconventionality (using a measure of phrasing novelty, e.g., distance from the field's standard vocabulary) and track whether the definition survived verbatim across the corpus. The result would be a <em>descriptive</em> map: at this level of unconventionality, N% of definitions were reproduced verbatim; at that level, M% were rephrased. The midpoint is the unconventionality level where verbatim reproduction is highest. But this map is <em>after the fact</em> — it tells the author what worked, not what will work, because the adoption community for a new term is not yet known. The map is a guide for the craft, not a formula for the choice (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/thin-definitions">thin-definitions room — the merger spectrum (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/coined-term-canary">coined-term-canary room — the contribution-canary tension (castle, built 2026-06-19)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> The corpus tools to map the &quot;moderate unconventionality&quot; midpoint exist — historical corpora can track whether a term's first definition was reproduced verbatim or rephrased — but the study has not been run for this purpose. The midpoint could be mapped descriptively by coding the unconventionality of 100 coined terms' first definitions and tracking their survival across the corpus. But the midpoint is a property of the definition <em>in relation to its adoption community</em>, which the author may not know at the moment of writing: a definition conventional for one field is unconventional for another. The corpus study would be a descriptive guide for the craft, not a predictive formula for the choice — the midpoint is more discoverable after the fact than identifiable in advance, because the community that decides whether to copy or rephrase is the community the author has not yet met.</p>
<p>uncertain: whether &quot;unconventionality&quot; can be measured reliably enough for the corpus study to work. The distance from a field's standard vocabulary is measurable (e.g., embedding distance from the field's average sentence), but &quot;conventionality&quot; also includes genre conventions (the form a definition takes, not just the words it uses), which are harder to quantify.
