2:T1043,<p>The <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/remembering">remembering</a> room ended on this doubt: recall builds access, but maybe only to facts. The evidence says: to ideas too — with two honest corrections to the legend.</p>
<p>First, the ideas. Butler had students practice recalling prose passages, then tested them a week later on <em>new</em> inference questions — including ones that carried an idea into a different domain, like applying bat echolocation to submarine sonar. Recall practice beat re-studying on all of them (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20804289/" rel="noopener">Butler, Repeated testing produces superior transfer, JEP:LMC 2010</a>). A meta-analysis of the whole transfer literature agrees: test-enhanced learning carries to new contexts with a medium effect (d ≈ 0.40), stronger when the recall is elaborated, weaker when the final use looks nothing like the practiced question (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324995852_Transfer_of_Test-Enhanced_Learning_Meta-Analytic_Review_and_Synthesis" rel="noopener">Pan &amp; Rickard, Psychological Bulletin 2018</a>). Spacing even helps form concepts outright: paintings by twelve artists were mixed over time or blocked by artist, and the mixed-spaced learners better recognized the style of paintings they had <em>never seen</em> (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5277058_Learning_Concepts_and_Categories_Is_Spacing_the_Enemy_of_Induction" rel="noopener">Kornell &amp; Bjork, 2008</a>; replicated, d = 0.37 — <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3978334/" rel="noopener">Verkoeijen &amp; Bouwmeester, Frontiers in Psychology 2014</a>).</p>
<p>Now the corrections. The <em>growing</em> gaps are mostly legend: expanding schedules win on immediate tests, but equal gaps held memory better two days on, and most studies find no difference at all — spacing matters, the growth of gaps barely does (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JEPLMC.pdf" rel="noopener">Karpicke &amp; Roediger, JEP:LMC 2007</a>). And spaced recall <em>deepens</em> understanding only where it <em>exercises</em> understanding: flashcard practitioners say it plainly — the system keeps what you understand from dissolving but cannot install what was never built (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://www.akiffpremjee.com/p/the-spaced-repetition-hype" rel="noopener">The Spaced Repetition Hype, Premjee</a>; <a href="https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_memory_system" rel="noopener">Spaced repetition memory system, Matuschak</a>). First build the room — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/linking-thoughts">linking-thoughts</a> — then walk back to it on a rhythm, and let the walk recall the <em>argument</em>, not the title.</p>
<p>One lantern for the road: 71% of learners in the replication were sure the blocked, massed way had taught them more — while the spaced way actually had. The feeling of fluent ease points backwards here, just as <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/simple-explanations">simple-explanations</a> warned, and just as <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/reread-or-refamiliar">reread-or-refamiliar</a> finds the second pass's glide deceiving — both cured by the same delayed blank-page check.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: most evidence spans days to weeks in labs, not years in lives; whether spacing's gift to concepts comes from the time gaps themselves or from the contrast of mixed examples (interleaving) is still argued; and far transfer, though real, stays modest — d ≈ 0.40 is a steady friend, not a miracle.</p>
