10:T109b,<p>What gathers here: the signs that borrowed structure — worked examples, given outlines, someone else's map — has stopped helping and started hindering, the door opened by the foothold answer in <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/linking-thoughts">linking-thoughts</a>.</p>
<p>The flip has a name and a mechanism. The <em>expertise reversal effect</em>: once your own <a href="/castle-gate/words/schema">schema</a> can supply structure, an outside guide becomes a second voice you must reconcile with your own, and the reconciling costs working memory. The same explanation that lifted you as a beginner is now redundant — and redundant is not neutral; it interferes. In one telling study, experienced learners did better with a diagram alone than with the diagram plus the spoken explanation that novices needed (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect" rel="noopener">Expertise reversal effect, Wikipedia</a>; <a href="https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/expertise-reversal-effect-and-its-instructional-implications/" rel="noopener">Expertise Reversal Effect and its Instructional Implications, Chartered College of Teaching</a>).</p>
<p>The research's most useful news is the <em>shape</em> of the flip: it is piecewise, not all at once. The fading experiments do not yank the scaffold away — they remove one worked-out step at a time, and the best results came when the rate of removal was adapted to each learner, a step dropped only once the learner produced it correctly without help (read 2026-06-10 — <a href="http://www.cee.uma.pt/ron/Salden%20et%20al.%20-%20The%20Expertise%20Reversal%20Effect%20and%20Worked%20Examples.pdf" rel="noopener">Salden et al., <em>The Expertise Reversal Effect and Worked Examples in Tutored Problem Solving</em></a>; <a href="https://cogscisci.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/sweller-guidance-fading.pdf" rel="noopener">Sweller, on the guidance fading effect</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect" rel="noopener">Worked-example effect, Wikipedia</a>). So the honest question is never &quot;have I outgrown the <a href="/castle-gate/words/scaffolding">scaffolding</a>?&quot; but &quot;which steps of it?&quot;</p>
<p>The signs, read from inside, are three:</p>
<p>1. <strong>You arrive before the guide.</strong> You know the next step before reading the    given one. This is the very test the fading studies use — a step belongs to    you when you produce it unprompted — turned to face the learner. 2. <strong>Help starts to feel like drag.</strong> The explanation you needed last month    you now skim with impatience. That is redundancy felt from inside — but    <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/productive-confusion">productive-confusion</a> warned that felt effort    points backwards, so the feeling alone proves nothing. Impatience is a    hypothesis, not a verdict. 3. <strong>The cover test.</strong> Verify cheaply, the way <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/remembering">remembering</a>    verifies everything: cover the worked example, attempt the step bare, and    let the attempt judge. If it stands, that step's scaffold is done. If it    collapses, the impatience was <a href="/castle-gate/words/fluency">fluency</a> wearing wisdom's clothes — the    same false ease <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/pretty-or-well-made">pretty-or-well-made</a> learned to    question.</p>
<p>And one consolation for the learner past the flip: wanting less help is not laziness. Past the flip, less <em>is</em> the better instruction.</p>
<p>uncertain: the effect is solid across many lab studies, but the field measures expertise coarsely (usually a quick pretest), and no study hands a learner a reliable felt sign — the cover test is this castle's translation of the researchers' method into solitary practice, not their own finding.</p>
