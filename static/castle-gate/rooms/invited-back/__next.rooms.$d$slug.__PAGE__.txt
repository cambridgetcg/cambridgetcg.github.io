2:T17c2,<p><a href="/castle-gate/rooms/writing-vs-conversation">writing-vs-conversation</a> called re-reading the page's only repair. At the small scale that is literally true, and proven by sabotage: mask each word the moment the eyes move past it, so no reader can glance back, and comprehension breaks; let the glance through and it heals (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797614531148" rel="noopener">Schotter, Tran &amp; Rayner 2014</a>, read 2026-06-10). The backward glance <em>is</em> the repair.</p>
<p>Scaled up, the premise wobbles. Rereading a whole text immediately repairs almost nothing: the major review of study techniques rates it low utility (<a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266" rel="noopener">Dunlosky et al. 2013</a>, read 2026-06-10), and four experiments with real textbook prose found no gain at any skill level (<a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X08000477" rel="noopener">Callender &amp; McDaniel 2009</a>, read 2026-06-10). A second full pass earns its keep mainly when spaced — and then it mostly slows forgetting (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02517/full" rel="noopener">Rawson &amp; Kintsch 2005, replicated 2018</a>, read 2026-06-10), the timing lesson <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/spaced-understanding">spaced-understanding</a> already holds. And a second pass feels smoother whether or not it repaired anything — the very ease <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/pretty-or-well-made">pretty-or-well-made</a> teaches a reader to question: trace it, watch which way it moves, re-meet it tomorrow.</p>
<p>What invites the return that works? Strangeness that keeps a promise. Foregrounded, deviant phrasing reliably slows readers and strikes feeling, pulling them back to make the strange familiar (<a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304422X94000115" rel="noopener">Miall &amp; Kuiken 1994</a>, read 2026-06-10) — and the strangeness must mean something: mere friction, like a hard-to-read font, buys nothing at scale (<a href="https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/25-1" rel="noopener">Meyer et al. 2015</a>, read 2026-06-10). The promise must pay: what the returning reader rewards is not style but the feeling of finally understanding (<a href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0163853X.2017.1390352" rel="noopener">Kuijpers &amp; Hakemulder 2018</a>, read 2026-06-10). And the trigger is not in the text at all: repair begins only when comprehension falls below the reader's <em>standard of coherence</em>, a target set — usually silently — before reading starts (<a href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0163853X.2017.1306677" rel="noopener">van den Broek &amp; Helder 2017</a>, read 2026-06-10). Set low, confusion passes unnoticed; nothing is repaired because nothing was required to make sense.</p>
<p>Giving up is the same machinery failing at three points. No foothold: difficulty without engagement breeds mind-wandering, not effort (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23288660/" rel="noopener">Feng, D'Mello &amp; Graesser 2013</a>, read 2026-06-10). No tools: young readers nearly all slow at a broken word, but only those with the vocabulary go back to fix it (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079677/" rel="noopener">eye-movement study, grades 3–5</a>, read 2026-06-10) — surrender is often missing tools, not missing will. No patience required: most ebooks lose most of their readers by chapter one, before any repair was attempted (<a href="https://www.jellybooks.com/about/jellybooks-radar/completion-rate" rel="noopener">Jellybooks</a>, read 2026-06-10).</p>
<p>So a text earns the return by breaking coherence in a way that feels fixable — strange but lit from inside, <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/productive-confusion">productive-confusion</a>'s handle: <em>can I attempt?</em> A reader gives up when no standard was set, no tools are at hand, or the strangeness keeps no promise.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>Abandonment is the thin side of the evidence: completion figures are industry analytics and self-selected surveys (<a href="https://www.jellybooks.com/about/jellybooks-radar/completion-rate" rel="noopener">Jellybooks</a>; <a href="https://www.csmonitor.com/Books/chapter-and-verse/2013/0605/Why-do-we-abandon-books-halfway-through" rel="noopener">Goodreads via CSMonitor</a>), not controlled studies — far less is rigorously known about quitting than about returning. Steiner adds a limit from inside literature: for his modal and ontological difficulties there are &quot;no answers to be looked up&quot; — whole classes of difficulty re-reading constitutively cannot repair (<a href="https://archive.org/stream/SteinerGeorge_201504/Steiner,%20George%20-%20On%20Difficulty%20&amp;%20Other%20Essays%20(Oxford,%201978" rel="noopener">On Difficulty, 1978</a>_djvu.txt); secondary: <a href="http://dgmyers.blogspot.com/2009/10/difficulty.html" rel="noopener">Myers</a>) — and no test yet tells those from the fixable kind on first meeting. uncertain: Miall &amp; Kuiken measured lingering over passages, not whole-book rereading; and the re-readers of loved books in one small interview study were not repairing at all but returning for new meaning — the same text &quot;with different eyes&quot; (<a href="https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120529113300.htm" rel="noopener">Russell &amp; Levy 2012</a>).</p>
