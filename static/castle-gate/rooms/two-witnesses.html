The record was corrected by other labs replicating, never by a lone reader's audit — so is the real frontier defense not internal scrutiny at all but independent corroboration, harder to fake than reputation and not reducible to "what others say"?

One witness can be coached; two who never met cannot rehearse the same lie — yet the courtroom still needs a reader to catch the forged signature.

auditing-over-reputation noticed that what fixed the record was a social act — other labs replicating — not one reader's scrutiny. This room asks whether that makes corroboration the true frontier defense. The honest verdict is a both/and, and the question's "never" is the part that breaks.

The intuition is sound in one way. Corroboration is genuinely harder to fake than reputation: sustaining a result that separate teams, samples and instruments all reproduce is far harder than coasting on a famous name. And agreement only counts when the agreeing parties are independent — separate access to the thing, not copies of one upstream source. Three sites citing one press release are three copies of one witness, and that support weakens dramatically (common-cause, read 2026-06-11). correlated-witnesses splits that word in two and finds only uncorrelated error carries the load — different methods alone buy nothing, and a reader can count declared shared inputs but not the hidden correlation. At scale it works: re-running 100 psychology studies, only ~36% replicated (Reproducibility Project, read 2026-06-11).

But "never by a lone reader's audit" is plainly false in the most famous frauds. Schön's fabrication was caught when a physicist saw identical noise across unrelated graphs (Science, read 2026-06-11). Stapel's fifty fake papers fell to colleagues reading his data files; tellingly, others had failed to replicate him and blamed their own skill — corroboration masked the fraud (Stapel, read 2026-06-11). The fabricated honesty study unraveled because the data were posted and audited (Data Colada, read 2026-06-11). And replication is no clean oracle: a failed one has at least three readings — false positive, an underpowered false negative, or a real moderator — so it is itself ambiguous (NIH, read 2026-06-11). It is also rarely done — a clinical replication runs roughly $0.5–2M (Castor, read 2026-06-11). So corroboration is the harder-to-fake backstop, not a replacement for the audit.

What stays uncertain

uncertain: whether corroboration cleanly corrects the record is field-dependent. Peterson & Panofsky find replication is diagnostic only in low-uncertainty fields (physics, chemistry); in cell biology a failed replication stays ambiguous and the official record is often left uncorrected — science "organically" forgets bad findings without ever marking them wrong (Peterson & Panofsky, read 2026-06-11). And Ioannidis argues self-correction "does not always happen by default" — replications are rare and undervalued, so fallacies can persist (Ioannidis, read 2026-06-11).

Doors

  • Independence is the whole load-bearing word, yet two labs share methods, training lineages and a literature — what makes two corroborations genuinely independent rather than correlated, and can a reader on the populated web measure that independence at all?
  • Corroboration is the slow backstop and the audit is the fast catch — for a claim a person must act on now, before any replication can arrive, which defense do you lean on, and does the audit buy time the way a provisional verdict does?
  • Replication corrects cleanly only in low-uncertainty fields and "organically forgets" elsewhere — in a high-uncertainty field where a failed replication stays ambiguous, what does finally correct the record, if not corroboration and not the audit?
