2:T1315,<p>Confusion keeps no honest inner gauge; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fog-meter">fog-meter</a> found the self-read the weakest instrument measured. Does flooding do better? Look first at how the founding research decides who is flooded — not by asking. Gottman's lab marks it by the body: a heart climbing past roughly 100 beats a minute into diffuse arousal, where listening and empathy go offline (<a href="https://empathi.com/blog/what-is-emotional-flooding/" rel="noopener">empathi, read 2026-06-10</a>). The pulse is the criterion <em>by design</em>; the felt &quot;I am flooded&quot; is the secondary, doubted read. The field already answered the worry — it leans on the body precisely because the feeling is unreliable.</p>
<p>But the body is no clean rescue. Felt arousal and actual arousal track each other only loosely — within-person coherence near .37 (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8158438/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-10</a>). And reading your own pulse unaided is famously poor: in the heartbeat-counting task, true and reported beats correlate about .16, most people undercounting (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29944964/" rel="noopener">read 2026-06-10</a>) — a number shaded by what you already expect to feel (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29378285/" rel="noopener">read 2026-06-10</a>). The racing pulse stands in honestly only through an outside monitor. Worse, the two meters fail together: alexithymia — trouble naming your own feeling — now reads as a general interoceptive deficit, so the person whose emotional fog-meter is weakest is also worst at feeling their heart pound (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5098957/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-10</a>). No backup channel for those who most need it.</p>
<p>What flooding has, that confusion lacks, is a coarse, learnable somatic signature — pounding chest, shallow breath, clenched jaw, the urge to flee. So the practical move mirrors fog-meter exactly: don't wait for a verdict (the flood dims the judging mind), catch your own first bodily tell, name it aloud — &quot;I'm flooding, I need twenty minutes&quot; — then actually soothe, because the break heals only if you distract instead of rehearsing the fight (<a href="https://www.gottman.com/blog/weekend-homework-assignment-physiological-self-soothing/" rel="noopener">Gottman, read 2026-06-10</a>; <a href="https://www.simplypsychology.com/articles/emotional-flooding-relationships" rel="noopener">simplypsychology, read 2026-06-10</a>). Not more honest about how much — only, like an alarm, that you should already be out.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: no study here pits self-reported flooding directly against confusion to ask which announces itself more honestly. The one flooding scale measured was global self-report and was <em>not</em> significantly associated with partners' vocal arousal; its authors call for a real-time, state-specific measure (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7007326/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-10</a>). Whether the somatic tell can be trained to fire reliably mid-quarrel, unaided, is unmeasured — and the body-to-feeling link itself varies person to person (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4547010/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-10</a>). Noticing is not even the only weak link: a correctly-called break still fails if spent ruminating, and the same &quot;I need a break&quot; can mask stonewalling, told apart only by whether the caller returns (<a href="https://couplestherapyinc.com/stonewalling-in-a-relationship/" rel="noopener">couplestherapyinc, read 2026-06-10</a>; <a href="https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-psyche-pulse/202503/understanding-and-coping-with-emotional-flooding" rel="noopener">Psychology Today, read 2026-06-10</a>).</p>
