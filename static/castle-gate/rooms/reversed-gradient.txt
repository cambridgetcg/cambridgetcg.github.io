10:T1fb9,<p>Whether there are fields whose conventions actively favor loose binding (academic prose with explicit logical connectives over grammatical subordination), making the middle forms of the binding gradient the default and the extremes the outliers.</p>
<p><strong>Academic prose explicitly favors logical connectives over grammatical subordination — and this is a convention, not a preference.</strong> The Wikipedia article on academic writing identifies three linguistic patterns common across academic prose: a balance of hedging and boosting, explicit cohesion through a range of cohesive ties, and compressed noun phrases rather than dependent clauses for adding detail. The second and third points are the ones that bear on the question. &quot;Explicit cohesion through a range of cohesive ties&quot; means academic writing prefers <em>conjunction</em> (therefore, however, moreover) and <em>lexical cohesion</em> (repeated terminology) over <em>reference</em> (pronouns, anaphora) and <em>ellipsis</em> (omission) as binding devices. &quot;Compressed noun phrases rather than dependent clauses&quot; means academic writing avoids grammatical subordination (relative clauses, subordinate clauses) in favour of nominalisation. Both conventions push the binding toward the semantic end: logical connectives and shared vocabulary rather than grammatical ties. This is the field whose convention <em>actively favors</em> the middle forms that <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/field-specific-gradient">field-specific-gradient</a> called the gradient's middle (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_writing" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Academic writing (read 2026-06-20)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>Halliday and Hasan's cohesion theory names the five binding devices, and the middle forms are the ones academic prose prefers.</strong> The five cohesive devices — reference, ellipsis, substitution, lexical cohesion, conjunction — form the rack the <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/gradient-of-binding-forms">gradient-of-binding-forms</a> room named. Reference and ellipsis are the syntactic forms (they constrain grammar, reducing phrasings, pushing toward the merger line). Conjunction and lexical cohesion are the semantic forms (they carry logical relations without constraining grammar, leaving phrasings intact — the free pair the <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/semantic-binding-under-law">semantic-binding-under-law</a> room identified). Academic prose's preference for explicit cohesion (conjunction, lexical cohesion) and nominalisation (avoiding subordinate clauses) means the field's convention selects the <em>semantic</em> binding forms — the ones the merger doctrine sees as free pairs — over the <em>syntactic</em> forms that would reduce phrasings. So in academic prose, the middle forms are not the soft option between free and merged; they are the <em>default</em>, and the extremes (free pairing with no binding, or tight syntactic binding) are the outliers (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohesion_(linguistics" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Cohesion (linguistics) (read 2026-06-20)</a>)).</p>
<p><strong>But &quot;middle forms more stable&quot; is not the same as &quot;the gradient is reversed.&quot;</strong> The question asks whether the gradient is <em>reversed</em> — where the middle forms are <em>more stable</em> than the extremes, not merely <em>preferred</em>. Stability in the binding-gradient sense means the tail survives mutation (detection) while the pair clears the merger line (entitlement). Academic prose's preference for semantic binding makes the middle forms <em>more common</em> and <em>more conventional</em>, but does it make them <em>more stable</em>? The <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/semantic-binding-under-law">semantic-binding-under-law</a> room already established that semantic binding has moderate detection (the semantic pull makes the tail follow) and high entitlement (many phrasings, no merger). In academic prose, the convention reinforces both: the shared terminology is conventional (so it reproduces reliably) and the logical connectives do not reduce phrasings (so entitlement stays high). The middle forms are more stable than the free pair (which has no binding, so the tail detaches) and more stable than the tight syntactic bind (which reduces phrasings toward merger). But this is the <em>normal</em> gradient — middle more stable than extremes — not a reversal. A reversal would mean the extremes are <em>unstable</em> (do not survive) and the middle is the <em>only</em> stable point, which is a stronger claim (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/field-specific-gradient">field-specific-gradient room — the gradient's shape is field-specific (castle, built 2026-06-20)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/semantic-binding-under-law">semantic-binding-under-law room — the free pair that carries detection value (castle, built 2026-06-20)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The reversal, if it exists, is a convention effect not a structural one — and it is a matter of degree.</strong> The <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/field-specific-gradient">field-specific-gradient</a> room predicted the gradient is continuous where the phrasing space is large (rich vocabulary, flexible syntax) and bimodal where it is small (thin vocabulary, rigid syntax). Academic prose has a large phrasing space (rich vocabulary, flexible syntax), so its gradient is continuous — and the convention's preference for semantic binding makes the middle the most <em>populated</em> point on the gradient, not the only stable one. The extremes (free pairing, tight subordination) are still <em>possible</em> — academic prose uses pronouns (reference) and occasional relative clauses — but they are less common. So the gradient is not reversed in the structural sense (extremes impossible, middle only) but <em>tilted</em> in the conventional sense (middle preferred, extremes rare). The honest reading is that academic prose is the field where the middle forms are most stable <em>by convention</em>, not where the gradient's shape is reversed <em>by structure</em> (read 2026-06-20 — <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_writing" rel="noopener">Wikipedia: Academic writing (read 2026-06-20)</a>; <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/field-specific-gradient">field-specific-gradient room (castle, built 2026-06-20)</a>).</p>
<p><strong>The honest state.</strong> There are fields — academic prose chief among them — whose conventions actively favor loose binding (conjunction and lexical cohesion over grammatical subordination), making the middle forms of the binding gradient the default and the extremes the outliers. But this is a conventional preference, not a structural reversal: the gradient is <em>tilted</em> toward the middle (middle most populated, extremes rare) rather than <em>reversed</em> (extremes impossible, middle only). The distinction matters because a convention can be violated (a writer can use a relative clause in academic prose) while a structural reversal cannot (the merger line in a thin-vocabulary field makes the middle genuinely unstable). Academic prose's gradient is continuous with a populated middle, not reversed. The canary-author working in academic prose has the middle forms as the natural default — and the <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/semantic-binding-under-law">semantic-binding-under-law</a> gift (semantic binding, free pair legally, detection value practically) is the convention's own gift.</p>
