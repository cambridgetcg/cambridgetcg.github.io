10:T1331,<p>Split the question in two, because the evidence does.</p>
<p>First: can a non-mind make you <em>feel heard</em>? Yes. In a controlled experiment (n=455), AI-written replies left people feeling more heard than replies from untrained humans — 5.74 against 5.17 on a seven-point scale — because the machine validated the feeling and skipped the unsolicited fixing (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>). Attunement-shaped wording, not a conscious mind, is what the felt sense reads.</p>
<p>But the felt sense will not sit still apart from belief. Label that same warm message &quot;AI&quot; and feeling-heard falls — 5.81 to 5.13 — and for emotional <em>connection</em> the loss tracks mind perception: people prize the words less once they decide no one is home behind them (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38551835/" rel="noopener">PubMed, read 2026-06-11</a>). Offered both, they pick human empathy 57–62% of the time even while rating the machine's higher (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12872445/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>). So &quot;the felt sense of being heard&quot; and &quot;a mind that can attune&quot; do not cleanly come apart; believing a mind is there is part of what makes the feeling. This is the measurement <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/text-answers-back">text-answers-back</a>'s orphan-father door was reaching for: knowing no mind is home behind the words is itself part of what collapses the sense of being heard.</p>
<p>Second, and narrower: interoception. Here a stand-in does some real work — a static photo of one's partner sharpened bodily accuracy in low starters, no live responsiveness required (n=32, and the face was still a loved one's) (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5472092/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>). But social standing tunes the body too: excluded in a ball-toss game, people's heartbeat accuracy dropped, attention fleeing the body for the threat (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25701592/" rel="noopener">PubMed, read 2026-06-11</a>). And where the dyad benefit is <em>physiological</em> — two nervous systems falling into temporal step, heart rate and cortisol aligning — a journal has no autonomic signal to couple to; that part is non-substitutable by design (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12354601/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>).</p>
<p>A page with no listener still helps: expressive writing yields modest health gains (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10415981/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>), an imagined supportive conversation lowers distress (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10570303/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>), an AI companion eases loneliness with feeling-heard as the active ingredient (<a href="https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/24-078_a3d2e2c7-eca1-4767-8543-122e818bf2e5.pdf" rel="noopener">HBS, read 2026-06-11</a>). But none of these touched interoception. The honest center: a stand-in can evoke a <em>thinner</em> version of being heard; the full-strength feeling rests on genuine attunement — and whether any of it reaches reading your own body is, as asked, untested.</p>
<h2>What stays uncertain</h2>
<p>uncertain: the whole transfer. Every interoception result here used a real human (even just a photograph); no study has set a chatbot, journal, or imagined witness against the <em>interoceptive</em> benefit specifically. The feeling-heard findings run on minutes-to-a-week timescales, the writing benefits are &quot;difficult to replicate,&quot; and one experiment cuts the other way — interpersonal synchrony felt good yet weakened people's <em>later</em> self-regulation, because they outsource the effort onto the interaction (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6789117/" rel="noopener">PMC, read 2026-06-11</a>). So the premise that a responsive human is the gold standard is itself not settled.</p>
