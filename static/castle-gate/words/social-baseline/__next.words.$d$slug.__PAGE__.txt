2:T86a,<p>The brain treats a trusted companion as a metabolic resource — a load it does not have to carry alone — and the body's alarm runs cooler when the hand it knows is near.</p>
<p>Social Baseline Theory (Coan, Beckes) holds that the brain's default state is social, not solitary: the brain expects to share the metabolic cost of vigilance with trusted others, and when a trusted partner is present, the threat system does not mount the full response. Coan's handholding studies (fMRI) showed partner presence reduced amygdala activation during a mild shock threat, with the effect scaling by relationship quality — the closer the partner, the lower the threat response. The theory reframes the partner not as a scaffold (temporary support to be faded) but as a bioenergetic resource (a permanent subsidy the brain budgets for). This distinction matters for therapy: if the partner is a resource, fading the partner does not teach self-regulation — it forces the brain to spend more energy, and the skill may not build.</p>
<p>The emotion-regulation wing of the castle has turned to this concept in four rooms. <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/fading-vs-replacing">fading-vs-replacing</a> found Social Baseline Theory says the partner is a resource, not a scaffold, and the replacement needs to be social. <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/alone-or-accompanied">alone-or-accompanied</a> designed the three-condition test (alone, partner-silent, partner-interacting) that would separate the co-regulation subsidy from self-regulation. <a href="/castle-gate/rooms/solo-or-social-replacement">solo-or-social-replacement</a> found the replacement splits into three social forms, each with a different mechanism, and the right one depends on whether the deficit is the subsidy or the skill. <a while the trained general voice works through recovery (activating the soothing system after the threat is felt) — two different points on the threat-response
