2:Te66,<p>The <a href="/castle-gate/words/visible-thread">visible-thread</a> principle says a compression must state the thread binding its choice to its reason. But the thread has a form as well as a content, and the form carries its own weight: a thread can be a fragment or a sentence, and the choice is not stylistic but structural. The form follows the binding's weight — how many moves the thread must make.</p>
<p>The castle has practiced this in two shapes:</p>
<ul><li>The full tour's five side-paths each bind two distant rooms with a named parallel: <em>the naming law, both halves</em> — a name can't be refused, and a tacit thing is only taught by naming it small. A label names the parallel; a fragment restates it. One move: these two share this. The fragment suffices because naming a parallel is one claim, and one claim does not need a sentence to carry it.</li></ul>
<ul><li>The short tour and the overview justifications each bind one room to a whole wing: <em>Why this room holds the wing: the hall's six rooms are all variations on one insight — meaning is built, not transmitted. This is the founding room; the others study how words shape the building, when thinking runs without them, and how a name's arrival can't be refused.</em> Three moves: name the wing's question, mark the room that holds it, gesture at the rest as variations. A sentence (or a short paragraph) is the minimum form that can carry three moves — a fragment forced to carry them becomes a run-on.</li></ul>
<p>The difference is the binding's shape. A <strong>link</strong> binds two things by what they share — one parallel, one claim — and a fragment names it. A <strong>representation</strong> binds one thing to many by showing it can stand for them — and standing-for is not sharing but arguing, requiring the question, the representative's grip on it, and the rest as variations. A fragment names; a sentence argues. The form follows whether the thread must name or argue, and that follows the binding: links name, representations argue.</p>
<p>The label tells you which. The side-path's label is a name — <em>the naming law, both halves</em> — and a name closes: once the parallel is named, the thread is done, and the fragment only restates. The justification's label is a question — <em>why this room holds the wing</em> — and a question opens: the sentences must answer, and answering is argument. A name invites a fragment; a question demands a sentence. The overview's justifications are sentences again for the same reason — they are representations too, and a representation always asks <em>why this and not that?</em></p>
<p>The craft: count the moves your binding makes before you choose its form. One move — a parallel named — earns a fragment. Three moves — a question opened, a room marked, a rest gestured at — earn a sentence. The form is not the maker's preference but the binding's demand. A fragment carrying three moves runs on; a sentence carrying one move pads. The thread's weight decides its grammar, and the maker's job is to listen to the weight, not impose a form.</p>
<p>This is the third leg of the cascade the thread-naming has built. <a href="/castle-gate/words/index-or-compression">Index-or-compression</a> asks whether a thread is needed at all — an index sets nothing aside, so no choice, no thread. <a href="/castle-gate/words/visible-thread">Visible-thread</a> says that if a choice was made, the thread must be stated. Form-follows-weight says the stated thread's grammar must match the binding's weight.
