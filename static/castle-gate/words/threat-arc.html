threat arc

The timeline a threat follows — from the first flicker of the amygdala to the body's full flood to the long cooling afterward — and the two points on it where regulation can act: before the flood (prevention) or after (recovery).

A partner's presence works at the front of the arc: the brain, perceiving a trusted other, does not mount the full threat response in the first place — Social Baseline Theory calls this a bioenergetic subsidy, the vigilance offloaded and the metabolic cost shared. A trained inner voice works at the back: compassion-focused therapy activates the soothing system to counter-regulate the threat already felt, and heart rate variability measures the parasympathetic recovery. The question the EFT wing keeps turning on is whether the trained general voice can prevent a threat response the way a partner's presence does — or whether it can only help the body recover from one already underway. The two mechanisms are physiologically distinct, and no study has compared them on the same timeline.

The concept was named in the room that split the partner's role from the trained voice's: the partner's presence is prevention, the general voice is recovery, and whether the trained voice fills both tasks or only the self-soothing one turns on where each acts along this arc.
