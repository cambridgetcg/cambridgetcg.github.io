WORD · brick

self-determination-theory

A theory that says three things feed the inner engine of motivation: feeling you chose the action (autonomy), feeling you can do it (competence), and feeling close to the people you do it with (relatedness) — and when all three are present, a task you were told to do can become a task you own.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT), developed by Deci and Ryan, holds that human motivation exists on a continuum from external regulation (doing it for a reward or to avoid punishment) through introjected (doing it to feel worthy) and identified (doing it because you see its value) to intrinsic motivation (doing it for its own sake). The theory names three basic psychological needs whose satisfaction moves motivation along this continuum: autonomy (the action is self-endorsed, not forced), competence (the action stretches the ability without overwhelming it), and relatedness (the action connects you to others who care). When a teacher provides a meaningful rationale, acknowledges the learner's feelings, and leaves a real choice, the learner can internalize the value of the task — moving from external regulation to identified regulation, where the task's value is owned, not merely complied with.

The theory is the generating engine behind several of the castle's rooms: rationale-before-difficulty (the rationale is the autonomy-supportive content that moves motivation inward), chain-not-sum (acknowledgment in instruction may work through relatedness, not credibility), warmth-without-value (warmth helps you own a value the task already carries, but cannot create one it lacks), revealing-vs-creating (warmth is a lens that reveals hidden value, not a fuel that creates absent value), and the free-choice diagnostic wing (willingness-persistence-gap, inverted-diagnostic) where the gap between willingness and persistence is the signature of internalization that did or did not take hold.
